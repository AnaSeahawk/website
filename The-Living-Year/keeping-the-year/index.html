The calendar names it. The sky locates it.

A date at the top of a page usually says only what the calendar says. Calendars change; they have been reformed, renamed and moved by whole days. The Sun and the Moon are the clock beneath them all. They keep the count for us. The day, the month and the year are theirs, and every calendar is built on them. Where the Sun stands in its year, how far the Moon has travelled from it, and the place on Earth where the page was written: together they mark a day that any later reader can find again in the sky.

The old makers worked by this count. The alchemist marked the season of a work by the Sun. The perfumer watched the Moon for the gathering of flowers, and the gardener for sowing and harvest.

Keeping the Year is a Circle for learning to write that line. With the Soli Lunar Guide in hand, each person finds the Sun’s degree, the Moon’s phase and the year, and dates a first page by them.

What the Circle holds

  • ☉
    The year as a circle, a wave and a figure of eight

    Three hundred and sixty degrees from the first of Aries, and the four gates where the seasons turn. The Sun’s climb and descent, drawn as a wave. And the analemma: marked at the same clock hour from the same place through a whole year, the Sun, or the tip of a shadow, traces a figure of eight. Its height follows the seasons. Its sway from side to side is the gap between solar noon and the clock.

  • ☽
    The Moon in relation to the Sun

    The phases as a distance: the new Moon when the two stand together, the full Moon when they stand opposite, and the lunation as a second ring inside the year.

  • ⌖
    Where the day stands

    Today’s Sun and Moon, found together. Solar noon at each person’s own place, set beside the noon of the clock.

  • ✦
    A year of the world

    Anno Mundi runs from one entry of the Sun into Aries to the next. From that entry to 31 December, it is the common year plus 3894; from 1 January until the Sun returns to Aries, plus 3893. 2026 is 5920, and 5921 begins on 20 March 2027.

  • ✶
    Where each person began

    For anyone who wishes: a birth date, time and place give the Sun’s degree and the Moon’s on that day. The counting is done together, and each person fills in their own as it goes.

  • ✎
    The first entry

    Everyone dates and writes one page in their own notebook, there in the Circle.

At each season’s gate

The Circle opens every season, at the gate where the Sun enters its sign. The spring Circle falls in the seventeen days before the year’s first new moon, so the first lunation is written from its start.

Each one is complete on its own. Coming once is enough to keep a notebook for the whole year; returning at the next gate brings the season’s observations into the Circle.

Taking part

Any notebook will do: blank, lined, small enough to carry. Bring a pencil, and the Soli Lunar Guide, printed or on a screen. It is free, and arrives by email. No knowledge of astronomy or astrology is needed; the guide does the counting. Anyone who wishes to find where they began can bring a birth date, time and place.

The Circle closes with open conversation: a time to share what the sky and the season are bringing, as much as to ask questions. The conversation moves around the whole circle, among everyone there.
