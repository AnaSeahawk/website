Why keep these dates?

August 2 and December 19 sit on two different pages of a calendar. In this example, they mark two successive Phoenix passages. Put the pages beside each other and a stretch of life comes into view. What was underway in August? What has changed by December? A passage gives those questions a date to return to.

The Sun sets the marker. A line in a journal gives it something to hold. It can be as ordinary as the place you were living, a piece of work you had just begun, or a question you were still carrying.

How astrologers timed a life

In the second century, Ptolemy asked when the things described in a birth chart might unfold. In the Tetrabiblos, he considered long stretches of life alongside years, months, and days. He insisted that a reading fit the age of the person. What is possible in infancy differs from what is possible in adulthood. To read the time well was to see the person at that moment in their life. Ptolemy, Tetrabiblos IV.10

In the ninth century, the Persian astrologer Abū Maʿshar treated the Sun’s yearly return to its birth position as one way to read the coming year. The astrologer compared the sky at that return with the birth chart, looking for which concerns of a life, such as work or relationships, might come forward and when. The solar return was one part of a larger practice of timing. Abū Maʿshar’s method, introduced by Benjamin Dykes

A solar return comes each year when the Sun reaches its birth position. The Personal Phoenix begins at that same position and sets a new point every 138° along the zodiac. The number comes through Jason Breshears’s Archaix chronology, where 138 names a proposed interval in years. In this practice it is an angle, and each crossing gives us a fresh point from which to take stock. Archaix’s proposed 138-year interval

The Phoenix, step by step

1. Where it begins

Take November 2, 1974, as the birth date for this example. At the time of birth, the Sun stood at 10º Scorpio. The open circle marks that starting point.

2. The first Phoenix passage

Measure 138° forward from the natal Sun along the zodiac. That brings us to 28º Pisces. The first Phoenix passage came when the Sun reached that point on March 18, 1975, at about 8:36 p.m. Saskatchewan time. A person born on the example date would have been four and a half months old.

The point is a place on the zodiac. The passage is the moment the Sun gets there. The Sun’s apparent pace varies through the year, so the days between passages vary, too.

3. The points become a pattern

From Pisces, another 138° lands in Leo. The Sun reached that second point in August and the third, in Capricorn, in December. Each point is the starting position for the next step.

The wheel closes where it began at passage 60. In this example, that was in 1997. Passage 120 came in 2020, after the Sun made another twenty-three turns of the zodiac.

4. A resemblance in the sunflower

The golden angle is about 137.508°. Rounded to the nearest degree, it is 138°. In the sunflower model, each new point sits a little farther from the centre. On the Phoenix wheel, every point stays on the same rim. The two figures begin with nearly the same turn.

5. Why Oaxaca appears

On December 19, the Sun reaches the 136th point in this example, at 28º Sagittarius. The slide shows that passage from Oaxaca, where the local clock will read about 12:42 p.m.

A move changes where we mark a passage. Use the place where you’ll be at that time to find the local date and time. That place also sets the horizon and houses if you cast a chart. The Sun reaches the point at the same instant wherever you are.

6. What the points are for

The previous passage in this example was August 2 in UTC. Put that date beside December 19 and there’s a stretch of life to look back on. What were you working on around your last passage? What did you keep returning to? What has taken shape since?

The passage gives you a date to return to. What you find there belongs to the life you’ve been living.

Calculation notes

Swiss Ephemeris 2.10.03, with Swiss data files, gives the apparent geocentric tropical Sun. The starting longitude is the value recorded in the natal chart, 9°52′26″ Scorpio. The crossings were solved in order from birth through the ten-year extension. The first table ends at the most recent passage as of November 25, 2026. The extension covers the following ten years from that passage. Dates are shown to the minute in UTC.
