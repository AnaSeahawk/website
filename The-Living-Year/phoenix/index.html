A personal solar cycle

The Phoenix begins with the Sun at the moment of birth. From that position, a succession of 138° solar passages unfolds throughout a lifetime.

Each passage marks a moment from which to observe what has changed, what has taken shape and what is still becoming.

The geometry provides the rhythm. Life gives each passage its meaning.

Why keep these dates?

August 2 and December 19 sit on different pages of a calendar. They are two successive passages in my Phoenix. Laid side by side, the two pages open a stretch of life: what was underway in August, and what has changed by December.

The Sun sets the marker. A passage gives those questions a date to return to.

The Phoenix, step by step

1. Where it begins

I was born on November 2, 1974, in Unity, Saskatchewan. The Sun stood in the tenth degree of Scorpio.

This is where my Phoenix begins.

2. The first Phoenix passage

Measure 138° forward from the natal Sun along the zodiac. That brings us to 28º Pisces. My first Phoenix passage came when the Sun reached that point on March 18, 1975, at about 8:36 p.m. Saskatchewan time. I was four and a half months old.

The point is a place on the zodiac. The passage is the moment the Sun gets there. The Sun’s apparent pace varies through the year, so the days between passages vary, too.

3. The points become a pattern

From Pisces, another 138° lands in Leo. The Sun reached my second point in August and my third, in Capricorn, in December. Each point is the starting position for the next step.

Sixty passages close the figure where it began, at the natal Sun.

When the geometry returns

A passage comes about every 140 days. Sixty of them make a longer rhythm: at 138° a step, the Sun covers 8,280°, exactly twenty-three turns of the zodiac. After those sixty passages, every point in the figure comes round again.

On December 19, I’ll reach the same solar position I first encountered at six, and again at twenty-nine. I am now fifty-two. The Moon and the planets belong to the sky of each of those days.

The geometry repeats. The life continues.

Because sixty passages are exactly twenty-three solar turns, the figure always closes on a Sun Return: the twenty-third birthday, the forty-sixth, the sixty-ninth. My first cycle closed on November 2, 1997, my twenty-third birthday. The second closed in 2020.

That much the geometry promises. What it could not promise: November 2, 1997 is also the day I associate with the conception of my son.

I had not seen the two together until now, building the geometry and looking back through the dates of my life.

Rudhyar often spoke of the seed as both the completion of a cycle and the beginning of another. I find myself returning to that image.

The geometry returned to its origin. Another life was beginning.

4. A resemblance in the sunflower

The golden angle is about 137.508°. Rounded to the nearest degree, it is 138°. In the sunflower model, each new point sits a little farther from the centre. On the Phoenix wheel, every point stays on the same rim. The two figures begin with nearly the same turn.

There the likeness ends. The Phoenix closes after sixty steps. The golden angle is an irrational fraction of one turn, so its ideal sequence keeps unfolding without ever returning exactly.

5. Why Oaxaca appears

I’ll be in Oaxaca for my 136th Phoenix passage on December 19. When the Sun reaches 28º Sagittarius, the clock there will read about 12:42 p.m.

A move changes where we mark a passage. Use the place where you’ll be at that time to find the local date and time. That place also sets the horizon and houses if you cast a chart. The Sun reaches the point at the same instant wherever you are.

6. What the points are for

The point on the zodiac stays fixed. The life around it moves. At each passage the questions can be simple: what was underway at the last one, and what has taken shape since?

The geometry gives the date. What is found there belongs to the life.

Origins of the Phoenix

In the second century, Ptolemy asked when the things described in a birth chart might unfold. In the Tetrabiblos, he considered long stretches of life alongside years, months, and days. He insisted that a reading fit the age of the person. What is possible in infancy differs from what is possible in adulthood. To read the time well was to see the person at that moment in their life. Ptolemy, Tetrabiblos IV.10

In the ninth century, the Persian astrologer Abū Maʿshar treated the Sun’s yearly return to its birth position as one way to read the coming year. The astrologer compared the sky at that return with the birth chart, looking for which concerns of a life, such as work or relationships, might come forward and when. The solar return was one part of a larger practice of timing. Abū Maʿshar’s method, introduced by Benjamin Dykes

The number 138 comes through Jason Breshears’s Archaix chronology, where it names a proposed interval in years. In my Personal Phoenix practice, 138 is an angle. I begin at the natal Sun and place a new point every 138° along the zodiac. Archaix’s proposed 138-year interval

Keeping the Phoenix

I’m beginning to keep a more deliberate record of these passages. Where I am. The sky that day. The work underway and the questions that keep returning.

A date and a few lines are enough to begin, as ordinary as a place lived in, a piece of work just begun, or a question still being carried. When a later passage brings the earlier entry back into view, the two pages show what changed and what was already taking shape.

This is where the Phoenix meets Ephemeris, the Soli-Lunar Journal. The ephemeris gives the position. The journal holds the work.
