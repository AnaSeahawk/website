Ways of Living

What happens to our understanding of home when we stop treating ourselves as separate from the world?

We are not separate from nature. We are part of it. We are not separate from the sky, the soil, the waters, or the living world around us. We belong to the world we see, feel, and taste. Even our dreams belong to the reality of our experience.

We cannot stand outside the living world to which we belong.

After years of travelling, I grew tired of being asked where I was from. Eventually, I began answering by stamping my foot on the ground.

“Same place as you. Earth.”

But people wanted something more specific.

“No, where is your home?”

I would bring my fist to my chest.

“Here. In my body.”

Two gestures, two different scales.

The first was expansive: Earth.

The second was intimate: Here.

One opened outward to the whole world. The other returned inward to the body. Neither excluded the other.

Home need not be a place where we stop moving. It is a relationship we carry with us wherever we go.

When I first set out on my nomadic journey, my son remained with his father. I remember telling him, “You can’t move away from love.”

The Earth doesn’t cease to be your home when you cross a border. Your body doesn’t cease to be your home when you change where you sleep. And the love between people doesn’t cease when they no longer share a home.

Distance can change how we share our lives, but love does not occupy a fixed location.

A bird belongs to the sky, but it is never separate from the air carrying it, the land beneath it, or the living body through which it experiences flight.

A human life is not so different.

We build houses, grow gardens, prepare food, make things with our hands, and enter into relationships with places and people. We learn from the materials we touch, the creatures we live alongside, and the rhythms of the environments we inhabit.

And yet, we are often encouraged to live as though life should follow a fixed and consistent course.

We develop opinions, defend positions, establish beliefs, and are expected to remain faithful to them. Consistency becomes a measure of character. To contradict ourselves is often treated as a weakness, a sign that we haven’t properly decided who we are.

But what if contradiction is not evidence of confusion, but an expression of our capacity to experience life from more than one perspective?

A person can love solitude and long for companionship. We can seek freedom while cultivating deep commitments. We can be certain of something today and discover tomorrow that our understanding has changed. None of these experiences necessarily cancels the others.

Walt Whitman wrote in Song of Myself:

“Do I contradict myself?
Very well then I contradict myself,
(I am large, I contain multitudes.)”

There is something almost ecological about this vision of the self.

A forest does not become less of a forest because it contains decay alongside new growth, competition alongside cooperation, darkness beneath its canopy and sunlight above. Its vitality arises through relationships among things that are different, sometimes opposing, yet inseparable from the larger living whole.

We, too, contain histories we have outgrown, ideas we have yet to reconcile, desires that pull us in different directions, and forms of knowing that cannot always be expressed in the same language.

To contain multitudes is to make room for becoming.

There is freedom in allowing ourselves to reconsider what we have been taught, to explore unfamiliar practices, and to change without disowning everything that came before.

A living person is not a finished statement.

Something else began to change as I came to understand my place in the living world. I no longer felt myself to be a foreigner.

Wherever I travelled, I was still of this Earth. The borders I crossed did not change that relationship.

And I began to recognize something similar in the things around me.

We speak of foreign substances, unfamiliar organisms, contaminants, and threats from our environment, as though there were a world outside nature from which these things had arrived. Yet even the materials we transform through our technologies arise from this Earth.

We rearrange them, concentrate them, and combine them in ways that can nourish, disturb, or damage the relationships upon which life depends.

Their belonging to nature does not make them harmless. Nor does their capacity for harm make them foreign to it.

This changed my relationship with fear.

I could recognize that something was harmful without regarding it as an enemy. I could choose what to welcome into my body and surroundings, what to avoid, what to cultivate, and what to leave undisturbed.

Fear gradually gave way to attention.

I have come to trust the extraordinary capacities of living systems to transform, adapt, decompose, and renew. Not because every disturbance can be undone, but because these processes are continually at work.

And we are participants in them.

The soil receives what falls from the trees. Leaves decay, organisms feed, roots grow, and what appears to be an ending becomes part of another beginning. There is no need to separate ourselves from these processes to care for them.

Care begins with attention to the relationships we are already part of.

How we build, what we eat, what we return to the soil, what we bring into our homes, and what we cultivate around us become expressions of that attention.

There is an art to this way of living. Not the pursuit of a perfect life, but the practice of making one. Working with what is at hand, noticing what thrives, learning from what does not, and allowing our understanding to change.

And at the heart of it is love.

The love that allows us to remain in relationship without possession. To care without being governed by fear. To recognize that our choices have consequences, and that our participation matters.

Love does not ask us to remain unchanged. Neither does belonging.

Perhaps this is what it means to live from the heart: to meet the world with attention, to act with care, and to leave room for discovery.

Ways of Living is more than a collection of practices. It is an exploration of how to live freely without losing our sense of relationship—to ourselves, to one another, and to the living world.

Food, plants, movement and the spaces we inhabit are part of everyday life. The writing here follows those relationships through practice: a city balcony turned to soil, a rented room read for its light and air, the body as the first home.
