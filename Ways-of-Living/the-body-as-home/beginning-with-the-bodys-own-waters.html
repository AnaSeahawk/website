Beginning with the Body’s Own Waters

BEGINNING

On 3 April 2022, an interview with Dave Murphy, known online as Allegedly Dave, opened a door into something that has held my attention ever since. He was speaking about Shivambu—the practice of working with one’s own urine. Something about his account caught me immediately. By the end of that day, my own practice had begun.

The unfamiliarity was there, along with the old feeling that this was something dirty. Yet curiosity was stronger. There was no stack of books beside me, no long search through other people’s opinions. The reading would come later. That first encounter was enough to make me want to find out for myself.

Even the name offered a different way of approaching it. “Urine,” “Shivambu,” “the body’s own waters”: each names the same substance, yet each can evoke a different response. Before anything else happens, there is already something to notice in that response—the recoil, the interest, perhaps both at once.

At the time, we had just moved, and the heavy lifting had left my wrists inflamed and sore. Both arms had undergone radial-shortening surgery following injuries that affected the blood supply to the lunate bones in my wrists. Intense pain had been part of adult life for years. It was familiar territory, and the move had aggravated it.

Those first days of practice included a three-day fast. By the second day, my wrists felt remarkably different: movement was easier and the pain had eased. The underlying history had not disappeared, but the contrast with the days before was striking. In my experience since then, the pain has never returned to the way it was.

That was what first caught my attention in the body. What unfolded beyond it was harder to put into words.

The fast changed my relationship with fasting itself. Some of the fear surrounding it fell away, and Shivambu began to take on a spiritual significance. Over time, that significance reached into emotional life and even dreaming. Something so ordinary, so routinely dismissed, had become an intimate part of how I experienced being alive.

Only then did the books arrive: John Armstrong’s The Water of Life and Martha Christy’s Your Own Perfect Medicine. They opened further questions and led into reading and research that continue today. Experience had given me a reason to look more closely.

A LIFE

A practice within a life

The beginning was immediate; the relationship has continued to unfold. Daily practice brings the subject into ordinary life, where food, travel, menstruation, privacy, and changing circumstances all have a place.

One of the things that became noticeable was how the waters varied with what I ate. Taste brought an immediate, personal dimension to something usually considered at a distance. Meals became part of the observation. Attention kept returning to the connection between what went in and what came through.

There is also the question of living alongside other people. A subject can feel natural and deeply personal to one person while remaining uncomfortable for a partner or family member. That difference deserves some room. An intimate practice does not have to become a household discussion before there is a willingness to have one.

Menstruation belongs in this conversation too. My practice continues through my moon cycle, with the way of meeting it changing according to how I feel, where I am, and what else is happening in life. That relationship has changed through the seasons of my life as well. It is one of the subjects we make room for in the workshop and circle, where there is time for the questions a short paragraph cannot hold.

Years of nomadic living have brought their own adaptations. Days in transit are different from weeks or months in one place. A practice woven into everyday life meets both: the settled routines and the departures, the familiar surroundings and the unfamiliar bathroom. The circumstances change, and the practice changes with them.

This is part of what continues to captivate me. There are questions that only become apparent over time, through living with something and noticing it again under different circumstances. The first encounter opens into a much longer conversation.

CURIOSITY

Room for curiosity

My beginning was quick and wholehearted. Someone else’s may be a question they have carried quietly for years.

Perhaps you have heard of Shivambu and wondered why anyone would practise it. Perhaps someone close to you has mentioned it, or you already have experiences that are difficult to discuss elsewhere. Even the discomfort can be interesting: what does this subject bring up, and where did those feelings begin?

There is room to approach those questions without committing to a practice. Listening, asking, and considering what you hear are worthwhile in themselves. Personal accounts offer something to explore; each person still has their own circumstances and decisions to consider.

Since April 2022, these waters have been part of every day of my life. What began with an interview has become an ongoing relationship with my body, a spiritual practice, and a subject of sustained research. Familiarity has given me more to wonder about.
