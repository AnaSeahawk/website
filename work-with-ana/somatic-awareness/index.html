SomaticAwareness

Attention from within.

There is a difference between moving through an exercise and noticing yourself as you move.

ATTENTION

The movement might look exactly the same from the outside. Within it, however, there is a whole experience: pressure, balance, effort, breath, hesitation, ease. Somatic awareness begins when we give that experience our attention.

The word somatic relates to the body as we experience it from within. Approaching movement this way invites us to feel what we are doing while we are doing it. A familiar exercise becomes a place for curiosity. How does my weight shift? Where am I supported? What happens to my breathing when something feels difficult?

The Turkish get-up offers a useful example. Moving from lying down to standing involves a series of changing supports. We roll, press, lift, kneel, and rise. Practised with attention, each transition offers something to notice. Perhaps we rush through an unfamiliar position, tighten our jaw, or hold our breath. Perhaps a small adjustment makes the next movement feel more comfortable.

We can pause there. Repeat a part. Explore another pace. The sequence gives us a structure, and our awareness makes it a personal experience.

That same attention can accompany dancing, stretching, working, travelling, and the ordinary movements through which we live.

THE EDGE

Boundary Exploration: Getting to Know the Edge

I use the phrase boundary exploration to describe something I do while dancing, stretching, or spending time in an āsana. Within a position, I explore the movement available to me: different directions, angles, and small adjustments that reveal how I feel and where my edges are today.

An edge is a place to become curious.

What happens when I turn slightly? Where does my weight go? How does the movement change when another part of my body participates? I might linger, move around that edge, or follow a movement that emerges from being there.

There can be many possibilities within a single posture. A shift through the pelvis changes the reach of an arm. A different placement of the foot changes the experience of balance. A small turn introduces a direction I had not considered.

Sometimes I discover more movement. Sometimes I become more aware of a range I already have. Sometimes I recognise that I have reached a comfortable stopping place.

Getting to know the edge includes noticing how I approach it. Do I hurry? Do I brace? Am I trying to achieve a shape, or am I still feeling what is happening? Can I move away from the edge with the same attention I brought to approaching it?

Greater range is one possible discovery. Greater sensitivity to what feels appropriate is another.

THE SPACE

Exploring the Space Available

Travelling brings another dimension to this practice. Sometimes I find myself in a room so small that there is no space for my full range of motion. My body is small, yet even then I might be unable to extend freely in every direction.

That becomes part of the exploration: Okay, how far can I go here? What movement is available in this space?

The room gives me actual edges to explore. In a balancing posture, I might trace the edge of a table, or extend my foot to meet a wall and then run it down the surface. I am noticing the boundary of the room alongside the boundary of my own movement: distance, contact, balance, and the adjustments happening throughout my body.

The wall becomes a surface I can feel. The table’s outline gives my movement a path. The space between objects invites me to discover how I fit and how I can move within it.

These are personal explorations, responsive to the surfaces and supports available. Contact with an object and relying on it to carry my weight are different things; noticing that distinction belongs to the awareness too.

A small space can make certain details especially apparent. There may be room to turn but not extend, to lower myself but not reach outward, or to explore a small movement with considerable attention. I become familiar with the room through movement, discovering its possibilities as I discover my own.

PLAY

Making Room for Play

Playfulness belongs here too. Sometimes I pretend I’m a cat moving across the room.

Crawling under a bed to retrieve something becomes an opportunity to lower myself, reach, and find my way through the space. For a moment, the feeling of doing a “task” disappears. I’m playing.

Imagination gives me another way to explore my edges. How would a cat approach this space? What happens if I move slowly, stretch in a different direction, or let one movement suggest the next? The furniture, the floor, and the spaces between things become part of the experience.

One day, washing the floor with a cloth became an exploration in just this way. Reaching across the floor, drawing my hand back, shifting my weight—I could pay attention to the whole experience. An ordinary activity opened into a conversation between my body and my surroundings.

Play can emerge within what I am already doing. Gathering laundry, reaching beneath a table, or carrying something from one room to another can hold a moment of discovery.

Curiosity, enjoyment, and the freedom to explore belong within my understanding of health. They give movement a quality I want to make room for: the pleasure of participating, discovering, and feeling alive in my body.

This spirit can accompany a structured workout as well. We can follow a sequence, lift a weight, or practise a familiar movement while remaining interested in the experience. Awareness brings depth to repetition. Playfulness leaves room for discovery even within something we have done many times.

HABITAT

The Body in Its Habitat

Our habitats participate in how we move.

The height of a work surface, the position of a chair, the placement of tools, and the routes between rooms influence what we do repeatedly. We develop habits in relationship with these arrangements, often without noticing them.

Think of someone absorbed in desk work, or a bicycle repairer leaning over a wheel. A task can hold our attention so completely that the position of our body fades into the background. We may remain there for long stretches, returning to the same position day after day.

Somatic awareness invites the body back into our attention.

How long have I been here? How does this feel? Is there another position available? Could something in the workspace be adjusted?

The same questions belong in the kitchen, the shower, on the stairs, or while doing laundry. How do I stand at the sink? How do I reach into a cupboard? What do I notice as I lift, fold, carry, bend, or turn? On a stair or a wet surface, attention also includes the footing and support available.

Sometimes we discover a different way to move. Sometimes we discover that a shelf, a chair, or a work surface needs to change. Our experience gives us information about both ourselves and our surroundings.

This is where I bring the language of biophilia into the conversation. Our own living bodies belong within our relationship with the living world. In thinking about a habitat that supports life, I want to include opportunities for movement, sensation, variation, play, and rest.

I also find a connection with Christopher Alexander’s attention to the relationship between people and places. It prompts me to consider a room through the life taking place within it: how we occupy it, move through it, work in it, and feel while we are there.

Where do I enjoy sitting? What invites me to stretch? Where do I feel cramped or repeatedly strain to reach? What small change would make daily life feel more comfortable or more alive?

A space can be considered through the experiences it makes possible. The body becomes one way of listening to the place.

ĀYURVEDA

An Ayurvedic Lens: Appropriate Effort and a Living Context

Āyurveda offers another language for considering this relationship between activity, experience, and daily life.

In the Caraka Saṃhitā, vyāyāma—physical exercise—is described in relation to stability and strength. The instruction to practise it in an appropriate measure, mātrā, places attention on the amount and suitability of effort. Movement is considered in relation to the person doing it.

Bala, strength or capacity, is part of that consideration. So are age, constitution, season, and current condition. An activity that suits us at one time may call for a different pace or amount at another.

I find a connection here with the question at the heart of boundary exploration: What is available to me today?

That is my contemporary expression of the relationship. Boundary exploration is the name I give my practice; these Ayurvedic concepts offer a way to reflect on its measure and context.

Āyurveda also considers deśa, place or region, and ṛtu, season, in the organisation of daily life. Climate and surrounding conditions matter. Bringing this attention into the details of a kitchen, a workspace, or a small room is a modern application of that contextual way of thinking.

Health itself has a broad meaning in this tradition. The Suśruta Saṃhitā includes the condition of the doṣas, agni, tissues, and elimination alongside a clear or contented state of self, senses, and mind. How life is experienced belongs within this understanding of well-being.

That gives me room to consider pleasure, curiosity, and play as meaningful parts of caring for myself. Pretending to be a cat is my own expression of that curiosity. Its value in my experience is the attention, enjoyment, and connection it brings into an ordinary moment.

CARE

Awareness as Everyday Care

Our emotional lives accompany our movement. We bring our mood, expectations, frustrations, and confidence into whatever we are doing.

We might notice impatience when a movement takes time, self-criticism when we lose balance, or delight when something becomes playful. We may recognise the urge to push further, finish quickly, or make a posture look a particular way.

This is where I see a connection with mental well-being: making space to recognise our experience while it is happening, including the ways we respond to ourselves.

Can I meet difficulty with curiosity? Can I pause without judging myself? Can I recognise effort without immediately demanding more?

There is no particular feeling we have to achieve. Some days movement feels settling; other days it reveals restlessness or fatigue. Awareness allows room for what is present. We can adjust our pace, look around, rest, or stop. Those choices belong to the practice.

For me, that attention is also part of care. Noticing discomfort, fatigue, or a movement becoming strained gives me an opportunity to respond. Becoming familiar with my edges includes learning when to change direction or step back.

We do not need to examine every sensation. Sometimes a moment of recognition is enough: I’m rushing. I’m holding my breath. I need to shift position.

That small moment offers a choice.

Somatic awareness is a practice of being present with myself in what I am doing. Boundary exploration and play are ways I enter that practice. My habitat gives it an everyday setting.

It might begin in an āsana, during a workout, while washing the floor, or in a tiny room where I trace the available space with my foot. Each offers a way to become more familiar with the body I live in—and to feel more at home within it.
