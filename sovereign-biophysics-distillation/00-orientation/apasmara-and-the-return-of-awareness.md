# Apasmāra and the Return of Awareness

*An essay on the heart, the covering of awareness, and the lineage of distillation in Ayurveda*

Ana Seahawk · October 21, 2026

## The continuity of awareness

Charaka defines life itself as continuity of consciousness. Near the close of the Sūtra Sthāna he gives the meaning of *āyu*, life, as *cetanānuvṛtti*: the unbroken following-on of awareness, together with being alive, being bound to a body, and being sustained (Sūtra 30.22). Ayurveda is the knowledge of that continuity.

Apasmāra is the condition in which the continuity breaks and then mends. Awareness leaves its ordinary place, enters darkness, and comes back. Ayurveda studied this crossing closely for two thousand years: what prepares it, where in the body it happens, what the person sees on the way out, how the return unfolds, and what helps the return.

Following that crossing leads through the whole architecture of the tradition. It leads to the *hṛdaya*, the heart, which Ayurveda names as the seat of consciousness. It leads to the channels that carry mind, to sleep and dream, and to the remedies Charaka used to rouse a covered heart. Many of those remedies were made from urine. And that leads, finally, to the vessel: to the long Indian lineage of distillation, where a substance is separated into its volatile spirit and its fixed salt, and where Ayurveda and alchemy meet.

## The gravest illnesses

Charaka names eight *mahāgada*, great illnesses, and apasmāra is one of them. They are named in the section on prognosis (Indriya 9.8–9): each becomes incurable when it comes with *bala-māṃsa-kṣaya*, the loss of strength and the wasting of flesh. To feel apasmāra, it helps to feel its company. Each of the eight takes hold of the whole body, not one part of it:

| Illness | Sanskrit | How it holds the body |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Disorders of vāta | *vātavyādhi* | Movement itself disordered: tremor, stiffness, paralysis, the body's governor failing |
| The going-away of memory | *apasmāra* | Awareness covered at the heart; the person falls into darkness and returns |
| Chronic skin disease | *kuṣṭha* | The surface corrupted from within, the blood and flesh beneath it disturbed |
| Swelling | *śopha* | Fluid that will not move, held in the tissues |
| Abdominal enlargement | *udara* | The belly filling, the channels blocked |
| Abdominal masses | *gulma* | A hardness that gathers and moves inside |
| Sweet urine | *madhumeha* | The essence of the body flowing out with the urine |
| Wasting | *rājayakṣmā* | The tissues and ojas consumed, the body thinning toward its end |

What unites them is depth. Each begins in one place and spreads until it holds the whole person: the tissues, the channels, the vital essence. That is what makes an illness grave in Ayurveda.

**Gravity in the doṣas.** Within apasmāra the same rule holds. Disturbance of one doṣa, vāta, pitta or kapha, can be met and treated. When all three are disturbed together, the *sānnipātika* form, Charaka calls it incurable (Nidāna 8.8; Cik. 10.12). So too when it occurs in someone already emaciated, or when it has become chronic. The more of the body is drawn in, the harder the return.

**What to watch for.** Knowing the company of apasmāra is also a guide to watching. The great illnesses share their ground: wandering doṣas, weakened agni, depleted tissues and a troubled mind. Signs of any one of them, alongside warning signs at the heart and in the senses, tell the physician how deep the disturbance has gone.

Charaka also calls one further condition a mahāgada, in the apasmāra chapter itself: *atattvābhiniveśa*, distorted judgement, in which the channels of mind and discernment are covered at the heart (Cik. 10.54–60). Suśruta keeps a different list of eight great illnesses, the *mahāvyādhi*, which does not include apasmāra. The eight here are Charaka's.

## Apasmāra: the classical definition

Charaka defines apasmāra in a single verse, and every word in it describes something a body can feel (Cikitsā 10.3):

> smṛter apagamaṁ prāhur apasmāraṁ bhiṣag-vidaḥ | tamaḥ-praveśaṁ bībhatsa-ceṣṭaṁ dhī-sattva-samplavāt ||

In plain words: physicians call apasmāra the going-away of memory. It is an entering into darkness, with disordered movement, arising when intellect and mind are flooded and thrown together. The Nidāna gives the same picture from another angle (8.5): memory, discernment and mind are overwhelmed at once, and the person enters a passing darkness.

| Term | Literal sense | What it feels like from the inside |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *apa + smṛ* | away from + remembering | The thread that ties this moment to the last one slips. You are somewhere, but not *here*. |
| *smṛti* | memory, recollection | Not only stored memories: orientation. Knowing where you are, who you are, what just happened. In the context of apasmāra, Suśruta explains it as *bhūtārtha-vijñāna*: knowing things as they actually are around you. |
| *buddhi* / *dhī* | discernment, intellect | The faculty that sorts: this, not that; safe, not safe. |
| *sattva* / *manas* | mind; also steadiness, patience | The settled quality that holds perception together. |
| *samplava* | flooding, a mass of water rushing together | The faculties are not removed; they are swamped and mixed, like a river over its banks. |
| *tamaḥ-praveśa* | entering darkness | A going-in, not a switching-off. Awareness passes into a dark interior space. |
| *bībhatsa-ceṣṭā* | disordered or repellent movement | The body moving without its usual governor. |
| *āvasthika* | occasional, belonging to a passing state | The darkness is an episode, not a dwelling place. |
| *suptavat pratibuddhyate* | wakes as if from sleep (Cik. 10.8) | The return feels like waking, not like being switched back on. |

Two words carry the whole teaching. *Samplava* says the faculties are swamped, not destroyed. *Praveśa* says darkness is entered, the way one enters a room. Both describe consciousness relocating rather than disappearing. This is the classical text itself.

Charaka places apasmāra immediately after *unmāda*, disturbance of mind, because the two share their causes. He describes four kinds: vāta, pitta, kapha, and all three together (*sānnipātika*). The combined kind he calls incurable. A fifth, *āgantu* apasmāra, arises from outside influences and is treated along the lines of outside-caused unmāda (Cik. 10.53).

## Hṛdaya: the heart

In Ayurveda the heart is the seat of consciousness, the store of *ojas*, and the hub from which life radiates outward in every direction. To understand where apasmāra happens, one has to understand the heart as the tradition describes it.

**The name.** The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad reads the word *hṛ-da-yam* syllable by syllable (5.3.1): *hṛ*, to draw in or receive; *da*, to give; *yam*, to go ([Swami Krishnananda's commentary](https://www.swami-krishnananda.org/brdup/brhad_V-03.html)). The heart is named by what it does: it receives, it gives, it moves. The Chāndogya Upaniṣad hears another meaning in it, *hṛdy ayam*, "this one is in the heart": the Self dwells there (8.3.3, [Müller translation](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe01/sbe01163.htm)).

**Charaka's heart.** (Sūtra 30.3–14; [Arthedashamahāmūlīya Adhyāya](https://carakasamhitaonline.com/index.php/Arthedashmahamooliya_Adhyaya)).

- Physicians call the heart *hṛdaya*, *mahat* (the great) and *artha* (the meaning, the purpose). These last two names are used only in medicine.
- The whole person rests on it: the limbs and organs, consciousness, the senses and their objects, the self with its qualities, the mind and what the mind thinks of. Charaka compares it to the central beam of a house on which all the rafters lean.
- Injury to it brings *mūrcchā*, loss of consciousness; its rupture brings death.
- It is the seat of the highest ojas and the *caitanya-saṅgraha*, the gathering-place of consciousness. That, Charaka says, is why it is called hṛdaya, mahat and artha.
- Ten great vessels root in it, the *daśa mahāmūla*. They carry ojas and pulse outward through the whole body, *vidhamyante samantataḥ*, in every direction.

**Ojas.** Ojas is the essence that keeps every being content and alive. It is the first essence of the embryo, and it enters the heart as the heart forms. Where it is lost, the body declines; where it is gone, life does not stay. The vital forces, the *prāṇa*, are established in it (Sūtra 30.9–11). One teacher Charaka cites, Kāṅkāyana, held that the heart is the first organ to form in the womb.

**Three words for flow.** Charaka names the vessels by their movement, not their shape (Sūtra 30.12): *dhamanī* from *dhmā*, to blow or pulse; *srotas* from *sravaṇa*, to flow or seep; *sirā* from *saraṇa*, to move onward. Pulse, seep, travel: the heart's reach is described as rhythm and current.

**The doṣas at the heart.** Later classical texts, chiefly Vāgbhaṭa's Aṣṭāṅga Hṛdaya, place a form of each doṣa there:

- *Vyāna vāyu* is seated in the heart and moves through the whole body at great speed, carrying everything outward.
- *Prāṇa vāyu* sustains intellect, heart, senses and mind.
- *Sādhaka pitta* in the heart is the fire that accomplishes understanding, aim and self-knowledge.
- *Avalambaka kapha* in the chest steadies and supports the heart.

**The heart as space.** The Upaniṣads go further. Inside the small lotus of the heart is a small space, *dahara ākāśa*, and that space is as vast as the space of the world: sky and earth, fire and wind, sun and moon are held within it (Chāndogya 8.1). From the heart run the subtle channels, the *nāḍīs*. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka counts seventy-two thousand, called *hitā*, and says that in deep sleep the self withdraws along them back into the heart and rests there (2.1.19). The Kaṭha counts a hundred and one, one of which rises to the crown (6.16).

**How this reads in the body.** Put together, the classical heart is not described as a mechanism at all. It is a centre that receives and gives, that pulses outward in every direction, that holds ojas and gathers consciousness, and that opens inward onto a space as wide as the sky. Modern instruments do register the heart's electrical and magnetic activity at the skin and around the body, which is how an ECG works. Ayurveda's own words for that radiating reach are prāṇa, ojas and vyāna: life-force, essence and the force that spreads.

## How awareness is covered

Because the heart is the gathering-place of consciousness, apasmāra begins there. Charaka gives the sequence twice, in the Nidāna (8.4) and in the Cikitsā (10.4–6):

1. **The ground is prepared.** Impure, stale or ill-combined food, irregular living, suppressed urges and a mind already clouded by rajas and tamas let the doṣas build up and wander from their places (*vibhrānta*).
2. **The doṣas settle.** They lodge in the vessels (*dhamanī*) around the heart and in the seats of the senses, and wait there, dormant.
3. **A feeling stirs them.** Worry, desire, fear, anger, grief, greed, agitation: a strong emotion pushes the settled doṣas upward.
4. **The heart is covered.** The doṣas flood the heart and the sense-seats together. The heart is *doṣāvṛta*: enveloped, covered over.
5. **Awareness goes dark.** Covered at its seat, consciousness cannot reach out through the senses. The person sees forms that are not there, falls, and the body moves without its governor.

Charaka also names the timing. The roused doṣas bring on a surge every fortnight, every twelve days or every month, and sometimes sooner (Cik. 10.13).

**The words for this.**

- *Hṛdaya*: the heart as the seat and gathering-place of consciousness, the felt centre of the chest.
- *Manovaha srotas*: the channels that carry mind. Suśruta and Vāgbhaṭa name *saṁjñāvaha srotas*, the channels that carry awareness, as the pathway along which apasmāra travels.
- *Manobuddhivahāḥ sirāḥ*: the vessels that carry mind and discernment, rooted at the heart (Cik. 10.58). Charaka names them when describing the related illness of distorted judgement, *atattvābhiniveśa*.
- *Āvṛta*, *āvaraṇa*: covered, covering. The classical image of obstruction is precise: the heart and its channels are covered, so awareness cannot pass through.
- *Dhamanī*: the pulsing vessels, named from *dhmā*, to blow or pulse.
- *Vibhrānta*: doṣas that have wandered from their seats.

**Reading it in the body.** Awareness, in this picture, does not go out. It is covered at the heart and cut off from the senses, the way a lamp in a covered jar still burns. That is why the return feels like waking: nothing was destroyed, a covering was lifted. Charaka lists heaviness or gripping in the chest, *hṛdaya-graha*, among the warning signs. The covering is felt where it happens.

## Before: the warning signs, and the dreams

Charaka lists the *pūrvarūpa*, the signs that come before an episode, and several of them are dreams (Nidāna 8.6). In the language of Ayurvedic pathology this is *sthānasaṃśraya*, the stage in which the travelling doṣas take up their seat and begin to show themselves; modern medicine calls it the aura.

| Sign | Sanskrit | In the body |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eyebrows drawing together | *bhrū-vyudāsa* | A tightening between the brows |
| Eyes moving restlessly | *akṣṇor vaikṛtam* | The gaze cannot settle |
| Hearing sounds that are not there | *aśabda-śravaṇa* | Inner sound, ringing, voices |
| Saliva and mucus running | *lālā-siṅghāṇa-prasravaṇa* | The body loosening its fluids |
| Loss of appetite, indigestion | *arocaka*, *avipāka* | Agni faltering |
| Heaviness at the heart | *hṛdaya-graha* | Pressure or gripping in the chest |
| Gurgling distension of the belly | *kukṣer āṭopa* | The gut stirring |
| Weakness, aching bones, body ache | *daurbalya*, *asthi-bheda*, *aṅga-marda* | A breaking, wrung-out feeling |
| Confusion | *moha* | Thought clouding |
| Seeing darkness | *tamaso darśanam* | Vision darkening while still conscious |
| Fainting and giddiness | *mūrcchā*, *bhrama* | The ground tilting |
| Dreams of intoxication, dancing, piercing, pain, trembling and falling | *svapne mada-nartana-vyadhana-vyathana-vepana-patana* | The dreaming mind rehearsing the fall |

The last line is the remarkable one. Charaka treats dreams as part of the illness's approach: the dreaming state registers something gathering before waking awareness does.

Each doṣa also colours what the person sees as awareness goes (Nidāna 8.8; Cik. 10.9–11):

- **Vāta:** unstable, flickering, rough, dry forms; reddish-brown or black. Trembling, grinding of teeth, gasping.
- **Pitta:** blood, fire, burning and angry forms; yellow and red. Thirst, heat, the sense that the whole world is aflame.
- **Kapha:** white, heavy, smooth forms. Cold, heaviness, a stiff body, a slow return.

These visions are the qualities of each doṣa, seen. Vāta is mobile, rough and dry, so its forms flicker and scrape. Pitta is hot and sharp, so its forms burn. Kapha is heavy, cool and smooth, so its forms are white and weighted. The inner image carries the quality of whatever is moving through the channels, which makes the threshold readable: the texture of what is seen on the way out tells what is moving.

## The return: waking as if from sleep

When the surge of doṣa passes, Charaka says, the person wakes as though from sleep: *doṣa-vege ca vigate suptavat pratibuddhyate* (Cik. 10.8). The episode is a *vega*, a wave. It rises, crests and passes on its own.

The Suśruta tradition explains the waves with an image from the fields. A seed lies in the ground through every rain, yet sprouts only in its season. In the same way the doṣas can lie dormant at the heart, and rise only when the right conditions gather (Suśruta, Uttara Tantra 61, as discussed in the Charaka commentary).

**How each doṣa returns** (Nidāna 8.8; Cik. 10.9–13):

|  | Vāta | Pitta | Kapha |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How often | Most often | Often | Least often |
| Going under | Quick | Quick | Slow |
| Coming back | Quick | Quick | Slow |
| Movement | Most: trembling, teeth grinding, limbs thrown | Moderate; a cry, rubbing the ground | Least; falling, saliva |
| Body | Dry, dusky, rough | Hot, thirsty, sweating | Cold, heavy, sleepy |

A quick loss and a quick return, with the person able to rise soon after, is the pattern Charaka gives for vāta and pitta. A slow, heavy return with long sleepiness belongs to kapha. Modern neurology draws a similar line between brief events with fast recovery and generalised seizures followed by a long, confused aftermath. The two are not the same map, but they are mapping the same ground.

**What returns, and in what order.** The return is a sequence, not a single moment: eyes open before recognition, movement before speech, speech before memory. In Ayurvedic terms, each faculty named in the definition comes back in turn:

1. **Indriya**, the senses, re-open: light and sound arrive again.
2. **Manas**, the mind, re-engages: what the senses bring is taken in.
3. **Buddhi**, discernment, sorts: where am I, is this safe.
4. **Smṛti**, orientation, re-threads: this moment joins the last, and the person is *here*.

The Charaka commentary describes knowledge flowing in exactly this chain: from the senses to the mind, to the intellect, to the self. Apasmāra breaks the chain at its root in the heart. The return rebuilds it link by link, and the last link to close is smṛti: the remembering that gives the illness its name.

## Sleep, dream and the junction between states

Sleep and apasmāra happen in the same place, by the same means. Both are the heart being covered. The difference is that sleep is a covering the body makes and lifts in rhythm, while apasmāra is a covering forced on it.

**Sleep in the classical texts.**

- Charaka: sleep comes when the mind is tired and the senses, tired too, withdraw from their objects (Sūtra 21). Sleep is tied to tamas and to kapha: heaviness, stillness, covering.
- Suśruta, as the verses are usually read (Śārīra 4): the heart is the seat of consciousness in embodied beings, and when tamas overpowers it, sleep enters. He likens the heart to a lotus bud that opens in waking and closes in sleep.
- Charaka on dreams: when the channels of mind are filled with strong doṣas, a person sees frightening dreams (Indriya 5.41). He classifies dreams by source: what was seen, heard or experienced; what was wished for or imagined; what foretells; and what arises from the doṣas (Indriya 5.43).

**Dream as the junction.** The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad gives a person two places, this world and the other, and a third between them: the dream place, which it calls *sandhya*, the junction (4.3.9). From there one sees both. The word is the same as *sandhi*, a joint, and *sandhyā*, twilight: the hinge between day and night.

**The four states** (Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad):

| State | Sanskrit | Where awareness is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Waking | *jāgrat* | Turned outward through the senses |
| Dreaming | *svapna* | Turned inward, among its own images |
| Deep sleep | *suṣupti* | Gathered in, without images; resting in the heart along the hitā channels |
| The fourth | *turīya* | The witness, present through all three |

**Lucidity read through the guṇas.** Dreaming is normally a state heavy with tamas: awareness is covered and carried along by its images. Lucidity is sattva appearing *inside* that covering. The witness wakes without the dream ending. Apasmāra is the opposite crossing at the same junction: rajas and tamas flood it, and the witness is swamped (*samplava*). Same doorway, two directions.

**What the junction teaches.** In a dream, it is remembering waking reality that ends the dream. In the return from apasmāra, it is the same act, smṛti re-threading, that brings a person fully back. The faculty whose loss names the illness is the faculty whose return completes the recovery. A mind trained to notice, at the junction, that it is somewhere else has practised exactly that faculty.

## Samprabodhana: Charaka's awakening therapies

Charaka's first instruction for apasmāra is to awaken. The heart, the channels and the mind, all covered by doṣa, are to be roused first with sharp measures (Cik. 10.14): *tair āvṛtānāṁ hṛt-srotas-manasāṁ samprabodhanam*. The word is **samprabodhana**: a thorough waking, a calling-back.

**The order of treatment.** Awakening is the first movement of a sequence, not the whole of it:

1. **Rousing and clearing** with sharp (*tīkṣṇa*) measures, to lift the covering (10.14).
2. **Purification by doṣa**: medicated enema (*basti*) for vāta, purgation (*virecana*) for pitta, therapeutic emesis (*vamana*) for kapha (10.15).
3. **Reassurance**, *āśvāsana*: the person is calmed and consoled once cleansed (10.16).
4. **Soothing** with medicated ghees and oils, taken daily (10.17–31).
5. **Rejuvenation**, *rasāyana*, for entrenched and long-standing cases (10.65).

**Urine at the centre.** Charaka's chapter uses urine by nearly every route the body has:

| Route | Sanskrit | What Charaka prescribes | Verse |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nose | *nasya*, *nāvana* | Urine of a tawny (*kapila*) cow, called supremely beneficial; also the urine of dog, jackal, cat and lion; herb pastes ground in cow's urine, five or six drops | 10.41–43 |
| Nose, oil | *nasya taila* | Oil cooked in four parts goat's urine with triphalā, the three pungents, deodār and other herbs | 10.43–44 |
| Nose, powder | *pradhamana* | Powders of long pepper, salts and pungent herbs blown into the nostrils | 10.45 |
| Eye | *añjana* | Herbal wicks ground in goat's urine, rubbed with water and applied to the eyelids; said to work like nectar in restoring awareness in apasmāra, poisoning and near-drowning | 10.46–49 |
| Whole body, sprinkled | *avasecana* | The body sprinkled with cow's or goat's urine | 10.39 |
| Bath and unction | *snāna*, *utsādana* | Bathing with cow's urine; rubbing with herb pastes ground in urine | 10.32, 10.39 |
| Massage | *abhyaṅga* | Mustard or sesame oil cooked in goat's urine | 10.32–36 |
| Taken in, daily | *ghṛta* | Pañcagavya ghṛta: ghee cooked with equal parts cow-dung juice, sour curd, milk and urine; Mahāpañcagavya ghṛta with some forty herbs, called "like nectar"; ghee cooked in bull's and goat's urine with rock salt and asafoetida | 10.17–26 |

Charaka states the result plainly. With these measures the heart is roused, the channels are cleansed, and the person regains *saṁjñā*, awareness (10.52).

**Reading the lineage.**

- **Sharpness is the principle.** The commentary notes that these preparations are mostly pungent, chosen to penetrate in small doses. To lift a covering, Ayurveda reaches for *tīkṣṇa*: the sharp, piercing quality. Urine, in Charaka's own description, is sharp and pungent.
- **The nose and eyes are doors to the head.** *Nasya* is classically the route to the head and the senses; *añjana* opens the eyes from within. Both act where the senses, cut off from the covered heart, are to be called back.
- **Every urine here is animal.** Cow, goat, bull and, for the nose, other animals. Most are worked into ghee, oil or herbal paste rather than used alone.
- **The ghees are a daily practice.** Mahāpañcagavya ghṛta is to be taken "day after day". The research notes in the Charak Samhita Online edition include a clinical study of it in apasmāra, reporting fewer and milder episodes over the course of treatment ([Apasmāra Cikitsā](https://www.carakasamhitaonline.com/index.php/Apasmara_Chikitsa)).

## Restoring the faculties: the three therapies

Charaka does not treat the return of awareness with substances alone. He names three kinds of therapy, and apasmāra calls on all three (Sūtra 11.54):

| Therapy | Sanskrit | What it works on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliance on the divine | *daivavyapāśraya* | Mantra, offerings, ritual, auspicious acts; the person's relation to what is beyond them |
| Reliance on reasoned means | *yuktivyapāśraya* | Diet, purification, medicines: the ghees, the nasya, the añjana |
| Mastery of the mind | *sattvāvajaya* | Steadying and turning the mind away from what harms it |

**Mantra for what comes from outside.** For apasmāra with an outside cause, Charaka says mantras and the like are useful (Nidāna 8.10). Where the disturbance did not arise from the doṣas, the remedy does not begin with them either.

**Reassurance as treatment.** In the treatment sequence, reassurance, *āśvāsana*, stands between purification and the soothing ghees (Cik. 10.16). Calming the person is a stage of the medicine, not a courtesy before it.

**Restoring memory by teaching.** For atattvābhiniveśa, the related grave illness of distorted judgement, Charaka gives the clearest instruction of all. Friends, well-wishers and teachers of dharma are to draw the person back toward *vijñāna* (understanding), *dhairya* (steadiness), *smṛti* (memory) and *samādhi* (absorption) (Cik. 10.63). Smṛti, whose going-away names apasmāra, is here restored through relationship and teaching.

**The rejuvenatives for mind.** Charaka names four *medhya rasāyanas*, rejuvenatives for intellect and memory (Cikitsā 1.3):

- the juice of *maṇḍūkaparṇī*
- the powder of *yaṣṭimadhu*, liquorice, with milk
- the juice of *guḍūcī*
- the paste of *śaṅkhapuṣpī*, with its flower

In the apasmāra chapter itself, *brāhmī* is cooked into ghee with vacā, kuṣṭha and śaṅkhapuṣpī, and brāhmī juice is paired with Pañcagavya ghṛta as a medhya rasāyana (Cik. 10.25, 10.62). Where samprabodhana lifts the covering, the medhya rasāyanas strengthen what lies beneath it, so that smṛti holds.

## Mūtra: urine in the Ayurvedic pharmacopoeia

Urine enters Ayurveda on the first pages of the first great text. In the opening chapter of the Sūtra Sthāna, Charaka sets out the substances a physician must know, and among them are the eight urines, *aṣṭa mūtra* (Sūtra 1; [Kaviratna translation](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/charaka-samhita-trans/page/69ef217fb3e2d3a0927f3067)).

**The eight urines.** Sheep, goat, cow, buffalo, elephant, camel, horse and ass. Taken together, Charaka gives urine five qualities:

- *uṣṇa*, hot
- *kaṭuka*, pungent
- *rūkṣa*, drying
- *tikta*, bitter
- *lavaṇa*, salty

**Its uses.** Rubbing and plasters, enemas and purgatives, sweating therapies, retention of stool and urine, antidotes to poison, dropsy, piles, abdominal lumps and skin discolouration.

**Each animal's urine has its own character** ([Kaviratna translation](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/charaka-samhita-trans/page/69ef217fb3e2d3a0927f3068)):

| Urine | Character and use, as Charaka gives them |
| --- | --- |
| Cow | Slightly sweet; corrects the doṣas, destroys worms, treats skin discolouration |
| Goat | Astringent and sweet; wholesome; corrects all three doṣas |
| Sheep | Bitter and cooling; does not aggravate pitta |
| Buffalo | Alkaline and purgative; for piles, swelling and dropsy |
| Elephant | Salty; for worms, skin conditions, retention, poison and kapha disorders |
| Camel | Bitter; for breathlessness, cough and piles |
| Horse | Bitter and pungent; for skin conditions and poisoning |
| Ass | For apasmāra, unmāda, and illnesses attributed to seizing influences |

The last line closes a circle. In the very first chapter of the Charaka Saṃhitā, before apasmāra has been defined, a urine is already named as its remedy.

**Urine as sign.** Urine is also a *mala*, a product the body releases, and Ayurveda read it closely. Charaka traces the channels that carry it, *mūtravaha srotas*, to the bladder and groin ([Mūtra](https://www.carakasamhitaonline.com/index.php/Mutra)). Medieval texts such as the Yogaratnākara developed *mūtra parīkṣā*, urine examination, including *taila-bindu parīkṣā*: a drop of oil placed on the urine, its spreading shape read for doṣa and prognosis ([review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3215423)).

**The yogic and tantric line.** Alongside the medical texts runs a second current. Haṭha yoga names *amarolī* among its practices in the Haṭhayoga Pradīpikā, and the *Śivāmbu Kalpa*, a text traditionally placed within the Ḍāmara Tantra, is devoted to the use of one's own urine, *śivāmbu*, "the water of Śiva". The medical texts use animal urine, prepared; the yogic texts use one's own. Both treat urine as a substance with a nature, not as waste.

## The lineage of distillation in India

Distillation is older than any still. Ayurveda's own texts trace it through three currents: the separation the body performs on its own, the mercury laboratories of rasaśāstra, and the Ayurvedic pharmacy of *arka*. Following them shows how a distilled urine came to stand inside Ayurveda.

### Separation in nature and in the body

No one invented distillation or fermentation. Water rises as vapour, gathers as cloud and falls clean as rain; fruit left in its own juice begins to work. People observed these processes and built vessels to host them. The vessel is the human contribution; the process belongs to nature.

Ayurveda describes the body itself as such a vessel. When food is digested by *agni*, the digestive fire, Charaka says it divides into two: *prasāda*, the clear essence, and *kiṭṭa*, the residue (Sūtra 28). The essence nourishes the tissues in sequence, each refined into the next: *rasa* to blood, to flesh, to fat, to bone, to marrow, to *śukra*, the reproductive essence. The residue becomes the body's *malas*, among them urine, faeces and sweat. Urine is born from this separation of clear from residue: the body's own division of the volatile and the fixed, made inside the vessel of the self.

**Fermentation and distillation in the texts.** Charaka's pharmacy is full of fermented medicines, *āsava* and *ariṣṭa*, made by steeping herbs in sugars and letting them work in a sealed vessel; he counts eighty-four āsavas (Sūtra 25). Fermenting and distilling share the same materials: a vessel, heat, time, and attention to what rises. What the classical saṃhitās do not do is name distillation as a separate pharmaceutical form. That naming comes later, in the rasaśālā and in the Arka Prakāśa.

### Rasaśāstra: the alchemy of mercury

The second current is *rasaśāstra*, the science of *rasa*, mercury: Indian alchemy. It grew as a separate discipline and merged with Ayurveda gradually over many centuries. Its laboratory, the *rasaśālā*, was built around vessels for heating, sublimation and distillation ([National Council of Science Museums, Rasashala](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/rasashala-ancient-indian-alchemical-lab/VAVBaP1RF0y-KQ)).

| Apparatus | Sanskrit | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Upward-falling vessel | *ūrdhva-pātana yantra* | Heats the charge so its volatile part rises and collects above |
| Downward-falling vessel | *adhaḥ-pātana yantra* | Drives the volatile part down into a lower, cooled vessel |
| Sideways-falling vessel | *tiryak-pātana yantra* | Sends vapour through a bent neck into a second vessel kept cool with constant water |
| Drum apparatus | *ḍamaru yantra* | Two vessels joined mouth to mouth like a drum; held to be the best for upward distillation |
| Sweating vessel | *svedanī yantra* | Slow, continuous moist heating |

These operations belong to the *saṃskāras*, the purifying processes through which mercury is made fit for use. The Rasaratnasamuccaya, a thirteenth-century treatise attributed to Vāgbhaṭa, sets out eight basic ones, including the three *pātanas* ([wisdomlib](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/ashtasamskara)). The Rasasāra describes upward distillation repeated twenty-one times in the drum apparatus, and grinds mercury with *navasāra*, sal ammoniac ([Rasasāra](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/rasasara-acarya/page/69d007050d5fb0766d60c544)). Sal ammoniac is an ammonium salt: the same family of volatile salt that European alchemists drew from urine.

Rasaśāstra taught that raw mercury is poison and processed mercury is medicine. Separation is what changes its nature. That idea, that a substance becomes medicine through being taken apart and refined, is the heart of the alchemical stream.

### Arka: distillation enters the Ayurvedic pharmacy

The third current is *arka kalpana*, distillation as an Ayurvedic dosage form. Its text is the *Arka Prakāśa*, a Sanskrit compendium on distillation traditionally attributed to Rāvaṇa, the learned king of Laṅkā ([Wellcome Collection](https://content.wellcomecollection.org/works/qb7eggtk); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravana)). Historians, including G. J. Meulenbeld, who studied the text closely, treat the attribution as traditional and the work itself as much later than the epic age ([Meulenbeld 1981](https://indianmedicine.eldoc.ub.rug.nl/24372/)).

What the Arka Prakāśa sets out:

- **Its own five primary forms**: paste (*kalka*), powder (*cūrṇa*), juice (*rasa*), oil (*taila*) and distillate (*arka*), placing the arka alongside the oldest forms of Ayurvedic medicine ([Amṛtottara Arka study](<https://www.crisiddha.tn.nic.in/SCRI_Publications/2.%202016%20-%202017/13.%20Dr.%20%20KN%20SUnil%20-Oth%2048%20Amrutottara%20arka%20(Jan%202017).pdf>)).
- **The *arka yantra***, the distilling apparatus.
- **Ratios of water to drug** that change with the material: how soft or hard it is, and whether it is leaf, root or seed ([Guḍūcī Arka study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13653024/)).
- **Arkas from plants and from animal substances** ([Gomūtra Arka study](https://zenodo.org/records/10578804)).

The reason for the form is the reason for distillation itself. Some medicines carry their power in volatile, aromatic parts that boiling destroys; arka was developed to catch them. The modern Ayurvedic literature credits arkas with acting at lower doses, with higher potency and better absorption than other forms, and counts them among the cleanest, most sterile preparations, which is why arkas are made for the eyes ([Netraroga Śamaka Arka study](https://www.jssayurvedacollege.org/pdfs/5-Dr-Neha.pdf)). Their qualities are light (*laghu*), sharp and penetrating (*tīkṣṇa*) and quick (*āśu*).

*Gomūtra arka*, distilled cow's urine, is today one of the most widely made arkas. In it the three currents meet: the body's own separation of essence from residue, the volatile separation of the rasaśālā, and the arka form of the Arka Prakāśa, applied to the urine Charaka named in his first chapter.

## What distillation separates

Distilling urine does not concentrate it. It divides it, and the division follows the old line between the volatile and the fixed.

- **The salts stay behind.** The non-volatile part of urine, largely inorganic salts, remains in the vessel ([NASA, volatile contaminants in recovered water](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720022478/downloads/19720022478.pdf)).
- **Urea partly breaks down.** On heating it decomposes to ammonia, which travels over with the water ([NASA Tech Brief](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19730000224/downloads/19730000224.pdf)).
- **The volatiles gather early.** The first fractions can carry more volatile compounds than the liquid that went in ([NASA](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720022478/downloads/19720022478.pdf)). Each fraction is different.
- **A cow's-urine distillate** analysed for a patent was colourless, smelt of urine, and carried small amounts of ammonia, benzoic acid, hexanoic acid and volatile fatty acids ([US patent 7718360](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7718360)).

So a distilled urine, what this archive calls pure plasma, is water carrying the volatile fraction, with the salts left behind. That is exactly what the arka form was made to capture: the volatile part that boiling loses.

In Ayurvedic terms the separation is also a change of quality. Charaka's urine is hot, pungent, drying, bitter and *salty*. The arka leaves the salt (*lavaṇa*) in the vessel and keeps what is light, sharp and quick. The same substance, divided, gives two natures: a heavy, earthy remainder and a light, rising spirit.

## Gomūtra: fresh, cooked and distilled

Ayurveda uses cow's urine in three states, and each state belongs to a different layer of the tradition. What ties them together is one classical principle: *saṃskāra*.

**Saṃskāra: processing changes nature.** Charaka defines saṃskāra as *guṇāntarādhāna*, the placing of new qualities into a substance (Vimāna 1). Among the means he names are contact with water and fire, cleansing, churning, place, time, steeping and the vessel. A substance worked by these is no longer the same medicine. Cooking urine into ghee is saṃskāra. Distilling it is saṃskāra. This is the Ayurvedic ground for the alchemical idea that separation transforms.

| State | Sanskrit | Where in the texts | How it is used | Why this form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fresh | *gomūtra* | Charaka, Sūtra 1 and Cikitsā 10; Suśruta's liquids | In the nose (*nasya*), tawny cow's urine called supremely beneficial; sprinkled over the body (*avasecana*); for bathing (*snāna*); taken internally among the eight urines | The whole substance, with all its qualities: hot, pungent, drying, bitter, salty, and sharp (*tīkṣṇa*). Acts at once, at the doors of the senses |
| Cooked | *gomūtra-siddha ghṛta*, *taila*, *varti* | Charaka, Cikitsā 10.17–49 | Pañcagavya and Mahāpañcagavya ghṛta taken daily; oils cooked in urine for the nose and for massage; herbal wicks ground in urine for the eyes | Fire binds urine's sharpness to a carrier, ghee or oil, which reaches deeper and more gently. Suited to daily, long-term treatment |
| Distilled | *gomūtra arka*; in the Arka Prakāśa, a *drava arka* | First written reference to arka kalpana in the Śodhala Nighaṇṭu, around the twelfth century; the Arka Prakāśa | Taken internally in small doses; in present practice it carries the same indications as fresh cow's urine | The salt stays in the vessel; the light, volatile part is kept. Cleaner and gentler to take, keeps about a year, and belongs to the arka form, counted among the most sterile in Ayurveda |

### Where consciousness is treated

- **Charaka, Nidāna Sthāna 8, *Apasmāra Nidāna*:** the definition, causes, warning signs and types.
- **Charaka, Cikitsā Sthāna 10, *Apasmāra Cikitsā*:** the treatment, opening with *samprabodhana*, the rousing of the covered heart, and closing with the return of *saṁjñā*, awareness (10.14, 10.52).
- **Charaka, Sūtra Sthāna 24:** the related states of *mada* (intoxication), *mūrcchā* (fainting) and *saṃnyāsa*, the deepest loss of consciousness.

The leading means of calling awareness back in Cikitsā 10 all work through the senses: *nasya* in the nose, *pradhamana* powders blown into the nose, *añjana* at the eyes, and *avasecana* over the skin. Urine runs through nearly all of them.

### What the distillation texts hold

- **No arka in the great saṃhitās.** Reviews of Ayurvedic pharmacy agree that arka kalpana does not appear in Charaka, Suśruta or Vāgbhaṭa ([Pharmaceutical review of Arka kalpana](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/arka-50334323/50334323)). Its first written reference is in the Śodhala Nighaṇṭu, around the twelfth century ([Arka Prakāśa: a critical review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362760498_ARKA_PRAKASHA_-A_CRITICAL_REVIEW)).
- **The Arka Prakāśa.** Ten chapters, called *śatakas*, of a hundred verses each, framed as Rāvaṇa teaching Mandodarī. It gives distilling ratios for 196 single substances and 192 compound formulas with their indications, and the still it describes is the *vāruṇī yantra* ([Arka kalpana overview](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/arka-kalpana-and-its-modern-aspectpptx/256914835)).
- **Three kinds of arka.** *Sthira arka*, drawn from non-volatile drugs; *gandha arka*, drawn from fragrant, volatile ones; and *drava arka*, a liquid distilled from a liquid, for which cow's urine is the example ([Arka kalpana overview](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/arka-kalpana-and-its-modern-aspectpptx/256914835)).
- **Cow's urine in compound arkas.** The Arka Prakāśa includes a compound arka of cow's urine with ativiṣā, devadāru, pāṭhā, kaṭukī and other herbs, and lists arkas against sleep, *nidrā-hara* ([A Critical Review of Arka Prakāśa](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393644923_A_Critical_Review_of_Arka_Prakasha_A_Comprehensive_Textbook_for_Arka_Kalpana)).
- **Cow's urine as a purifier.** In rasaśāstra, cow's urine is a medium of *śodhana*: poisonous seeds such as dhātūra are counted purified after soaking in it ([letter, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3117312)).
- **Present practice.** Gomūtra arka is preferred for its palatability and for long-term internal use, considered comparatively safe, keeps about a year, and is used locally as well as internally ([clinical trial, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC13527936/)).

The published reviews of the Arka Prakāśa do not list the indications of gomūtra arka itself. That answer lies in the text, read with Indradev Tripathi's Hindi commentary of 1995.

**What stays and what changes.** Across the three states one quality holds: urine is *tīkṣṇa*, sharp and penetrating, the quality Charaka relies on to lift a covering from the heart. What changes is weight and salt. Fresh, it carries everything. Cooked, it is bound into ghee and oil and goes deep. Distilled, the salt is left behind and what remains is light, quick and clean.

## Solve et coagula: recovery read as alchemy

Ayurvedic recovery and alchemical work follow the same arc: darken, separate, raise, rejoin, renew. The parallels below are a reading across two traditions; each column stands on its own sources.

| Stage | In Ayurveda | In the vessel | In the body |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Darkening | *tamaḥ-praveśa*: entering darkness; the heart covered (*āvṛta*) | *Nigredo*: the blackening, the mass undone | Awareness goes in and down; the outer world falls away |
| Separating | *śodhana*: purification; in wounds, cleansing before healing | *Solve*, *separatio*: dissolving and dividing the fractions | The surge passes; what was flooded together (*samplava*) settles out |
| Raising | *samprabodhana*: rousing the heart and channels with sharp, light agents | *Sublimatio*, distillation: the volatile spirit rises and is caught | Breath deepens, senses re-open, something lifts |
| Rejoining | *śamana*, *ropaṇa*: soothing and closing, the ghees after purification | *Coagula*, *conjunctio*: spirit rejoined to body | Smṛti re-threads; the person is here again, in one piece |
| Renewing | *rasāyana*, *medhya rasāyana* for mind and memory | The elixir; the work repeated | The threshold is crossed more easily next time |

Several of these links are historical, not only parallel.

**Indian alchemy joined them.** Rasaśāstra made rasāyana its explicit aim: the elixir and rejuvenation were one project. Charaka himself closes the apasmāra chapter by saying that chronic, entrenched cases should be treated mainly with rasāyanas (Cik. 10.65), and he pairs Brāhmī with Pañcagavya ghṛta as a *medhya rasāyana*, a rejuvenative for the mind (Cik. 10.62).

**Maria in Alexandria.** The European line of the still begins with a woman. Maria, called the Jewess, worked in Hellenistic Alexandria in the early centuries of the Common Era. The alchemist Zosimos of Panopolis credits her with the *tribikos*, a still with three spouts for collecting the distillate; the *kerotakis*, a closed vessel in which rising vapours act on metals; and the gentle water-heat that still carries her name, the *bain-marie*. Her apparatus is the ancestor of the alembic, and through it of every European distillation that followed.

**European alchemy distilled urine to revive.** *Spiritus urinae*, spirit of urine, was the volatile salt drawn off by distilling urine, identified with ammonia or ammonium carbonate ([Source Library](https://sourcelibrary.org/encyclopedia/Spirit%20of%20urine)). Seventeenth-century chemists such as Christophle Glaser separated urine into its spirit, its volatile salt and its oil ([Glaser on the volatile salt](https://labyrinthdesigners.org/alchemy-ancient-chemistry/glaser-and-the-unladylike-but-volatile-salt-of-urine/)). That volatile salt is the ancestor of *sal volatile*, the smelling salts once held under the nose of anyone who fainted. Charaka put urine to the nose to restore awareness; European chemistry, independently, did the same with urine's volatile spirit.

**The ammonium salts meet in the middle.** Rasaśāstra worked mercury with *navasāra*, sal ammoniac. European alchemy drew its volatile salt from urine. Both are ammonium salts, the volatile salt of the old chemistry, prized on both sides of the world as the agent that opens and carries.

**The tria prima in the flask.** Distilling urine sorts it physically into the three alchemical principles. The *salt* stays behind: fixed, earthy, the body. The *mercury* rises: volatile, watery, the spirit, which is the distillate itself. The *sulphur* is the oily, combustible fraction between them. Read through the doṣas, salt carries kapha's fixity, mercury vāta's mobility, sulphur pitta's fire. The distillate is mercury without its salt: spirit separated from body, which is exactly what makes it light.

### Rasa: one word, many layers

The word *mercury* returns again and again in alchemy, East and West, and it is never only one thing.

**Mercury in the vessel.** Rasaśāstra worked with real metallic mercury, *pārada*. Its texts give chemical operations: sublimation in the pātana vessels, grinding mercury with sulphur into the black sulphide called *kajjalī*, and repeated warnings that raw mercury is poison and only processed mercury is medicine. The laboratory was real.

**Mercury in the code.** The same texts speak of mercury as the seed of Śiva, and of sulphur, *gandhaka*, as the menstrual essence of the Goddess. Their union in the vessel is the union of the god and goddess, and the work of the laboratory is also a work on the body and on awareness. Western alchemists said it openly: *our* mercury, the mercury of the philosophers, is not common quicksilver.

**Twilight language.** The tantric traditions of India had a name for speech that works on two levels at once: *sandhyā-bhāṣā*, twilight language. It says one thing to the uninitiated and another to those who know; an ordinary word, a substance or a body part carries a second meaning beneath the first. Scholars have read the term two ways. As *sandhyā*, it is the language of twilight, the hour between day and night, where things are half-seen. As *sandhā*, it is intentional language, speech with a hidden aim. Both readings fit alchemy. And the first is the same word the Bṛhadāraṇyaka uses for dream: *sandhya*, the junction between this world and the other. Twilight language is the speech of the junction.

**Rasa as plasma.** In Sanskrit the word for mercury is *rasa*, and rasa is one of the richest words in the tradition:

- **Juice, essence, sap**: the liquid life of a plant or a substance.
- **Taste**: Ayurveda's six tastes are the six rasas.
- **Feeling**: in Indian aesthetics, rasa is the flavour of an emotion as it is tasted.
- **Mercury**: the metal at the centre of rasaśāstra.
- **Plasma**: *rasa dhātu*, the first of the body's seven tissues. It is the clear essence that digestion yields, *prasāda*, carried out from the heart through the whole body, nourishing every tissue that follows.

*Rasāyana*, rejuvenation, is literally *rasa* + *ayana*: the path of rasa. So in one word, the alchemist's mercury and the body's plasma share a name. When the tria prima above calls the distillate its mercury, the rising, volatile spirit, Sanskrit has already joined that spirit to the clear fluid of the body. The word itself is twilight language: it means the metal in the vessel and the essence in the veins at once.

## Āgantuja: injury and the skin

Ayurveda divides illness by where it comes from. *Nija* illness rises from within, as doṣa imbalance. *Āgantuja* illness arrives from outside: a blow, a burn, a cut, a bite, a fall. Charaka's chapter on wounds, the Dvivraṇīya, opens with exactly this division: wounds are of two kinds, inner-born and outer-caused (Cik. 25). Even apasmāra has an āgantu form.

In an outer-caused illness the order is reversed. The injury comes first, and the doṣas are disturbed afterwards, as a consequence. That is why fresh injury is treated quickly, before the disturbance spreads.

**Suśruta, the surgeon.** Suśruta's Saṃhitā is the classical text of surgery and wound care.

- *Sadyo-vraṇa*: the fresh wound, made by an outside cause and treated differently from a chronic one.
- *Dagdha*: burns, graded in four degrees, from a surface scorch (*pluṣṭa*) through blistering (*durdagdha*) and a full burn (*samyag-dagdha*) to the deep, destroying burn (*atidagdha*) (Sūtra 12).
- *Ṣaṣṭi upakrama*: sixty measures for wound care, from reducing inflammation through cleansing to closing and finally rejuvenating the healed tissue (Cikitsā 1).
- *Śodhana* then *ropaṇa*: every wound is first cleansed, then healed closed. This is the solve et coagula of the previous section, at the scale of skin.
- Suśruta's catalogue of liquids includes the urines and their properties, and urine appears among the agents of cleansing.

**The qualities that answer injury.** Fresh injury is hot, red, swollen and burning: pitta and rakta, fire and blood, aroused. Ayurveda answers by opposites. What is *śīta*, cooling, and *laghu*, light, calms pitta; what is *tīkṣṇa*, sharp, cleanses and penetrates. A light, cooling, quick-moving application on a hot injury is the classical logic of pitta-śamana, pitta-calming, applied at the surface.

The skin is also where Charaka's apasmāra chapter meets the body most gently. Sprinkling (*avasecana*), bathing (*snāna*), rubbing with paste (*utsādana*) and oiling (*abhyaṅga*) all reach the person through the skin, the largest of the sense-organs and the seat of *sparśa*, touch, which Charaka says the heart sustains (Sūtra 30.6).

## Rakṣā: protecting the one who crosses

Charaka ends the apasmāra chapter not with a remedy but with a guardian's instruction. The person prone to apasmāra must always be protected from water, fire, trees, heights and uneven ground, because these can take life in an instant (Cik. 10.66). His commentator Ḍaḷhaṇa, writing on Suśruta, gives the reason: in the darkness a person may fall into water or fire. While awareness is away, the body has no keeper but the people around it.

**Guarding the heart between crossings.** In his chapter on the heart, Charaka says the heart, its great vessels and ojas must be guarded above all else (Sūtra 30.13–14). The way to guard them:

- Avoid, especially, whatever causes suffering of the mind.
- Take what is *hṛdya*, pleasing to the heart.
- Take what is *aujasya*, building ojas.
- Take what is *srotasāṃ prasādana*, clearing and brightening the channels.
- Cultivate *praśama*, tranquillity, and *jñāna*, understanding.

This is the preventive heart of the teaching. Apasmāra is triggered by strong emotion pushing settled doṣas up into the heart; Charaka's answer is a heart kept clear, nourished and at peace.

**The pure therapy.** Charaka gives one test for any treatment: a therapy that relieves one illness while provoking another is not pure; the pure therapy relieves without provoking (Nidāna 8.23).

**Guarding the body during a crossing, today.** The guardian's role Charaka describes is what modern first aid formalises:

1. Note the time it starts.
2. Move hazards away and cushion the head; put nothing in the mouth.
3. Once the movement stops, turn the person onto their side so the airway stays clear.
4. Call emergency help if it lasts more than five minutes, repeats without recovery, happens in water, or causes injury.
5. Stay until they are fully oriented: until smṛti has re-threaded and they know where they are.

## The return

Charaka called life the continuity of awareness, and Ayurveda followed that continuity to its seat. Awareness gathers in the heart, which receives, gives and moves, holds ojas, and opens inward onto a space as wide as the sky. From there it reaches out along the pulsing vessels and the channels of mind to the senses and the world.

Apasmāra is that continuity broken. The doṣas, stirred by feeling, rise and cover the heart, and awareness enters darkness. It is swamped rather than destroyed, covered rather than gone. Sleep does the same thing gently and in rhythm, and dream stands at the junction between the two worlds.

The return is a waking. The physicians answered the covering with what is sharp and penetrating: urine at the nose, at the eyes, over the skin, cooked into the ghees taken day after day, and centuries later separated in the still into its light, rising part. They answered it with reassurance, with mantra, with friends and teachers who call memory back, and with rejuvenatives that make it hold.

The last faculty to return is the one that names the illness: *smṛti*, remembering where you are. Apasmāra is the going-away of memory. Its healing is memory coming home.
