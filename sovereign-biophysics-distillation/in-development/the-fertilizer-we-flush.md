# The Fertilizer We Flush

*On the oldest, safest, most abundant plant food in the world - and why we throw it away.*

---

There is a substance that feeds plants beautifully. It's free. Every person on earth makes it, every single day, on a reliable schedule. It carries nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and a spread of trace minerals in almost exactly the balance growing things want. It's gentle enough to handle with bare hands. And nearly all of us pour clean drinking water on it and send it into a pipe, on purpose, at some expense, to be turned into a problem.

That substance is urine. And once you see what it actually is, the way we treat it starts to look less like sanitation and more like a very long, very committed misunderstanding.

## The thing we mine is the thing we flush

Start with the part that sounds like a riddle, because it's the heart of it.

To grow food at scale, farming needs phosphorus. There is no substitute - phosphorus is an element, so you can't manufacture it or brew it from something cheaper. Every gram of it in every bag of fertilizer was mined out of rock, from a handful of deposits concentrated in a few countries, and those deposits are finite. Researchers have a name for the moment the easy reserves start running down: *peak phosphorus*. It's not centuries away.

Here's the part that should stop you. The only renewable source of phosphorus on the planet is the phosphorus that's already been used - the kind that has already passed through a living body. Which means the single most supply-constrained ingredient in the world's food system is something every human being produces daily and then discards.

We are blasting open mountains to chase an element that all of us are quietly, continuously making - and rinsing away with the cleanest water we have. If you described that arrangement to someone with no prior context, they'd assume you were joking.

## What urine actually is

Strip away the associations and it's simple. Urine is mostly water, and the rest is the useful part: the nitrogen your body didn't need, in a form plants love, plus phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients like sulfur, magnesium, and boron that many commercial fertilizers leave out entirely.

The numbers are almost comic in their tidiness. The urine a single adult produces in one day contains roughly enough nitrogen and phosphorus to grow about 400 grams of wheat - enough for a loaf of bread. Eat the bread, and the loop turns again. What went out of you grows what comes back to you. It's not a metaphor. It's the actual cycle, and it closes at the scale of one person and one meal.

## What the crop trials show

The research record is no longer empty. Stored, source-separated urine has been
tested directly as a crop fertilizer, and the basic result is steady: whole
urine can be agriculturally legible before anyone distills, refines, or
industrializes it.

In cabbage trials, stored human urine applied at the same nitrogen rate as
industrial fertilizer produced comparable fertilizer value, with chemical,
microbial, and flavor quality assessed in the crop. In red beet trials, urine
alone and urine combined with wood ash increased root biomass compared with
mineral fertilizer, while protein, betanin, microbial quality, and chemical
quality remained broadly comparable to the mineral-fertilized crop.

The pattern also appears outside vegetable trials. Rich Earth Institute field
work, reported through a SARE project, found that undiluted urine increased
second-cut hay yield without visible ammonia damage to the grass. A 50/50
urine-water treatment yielded more hay in one trial, but the difference was not
statistically significant, so that part remains a question for larger trials.

The review literature keeps the boundary visible. Urine-based fertilizers often
show high nitrogen fertilizer value, but they are not problem-free. Salt load,
ammonia volatilization, odor, pharmaceuticals, pathogens, application logistics,
crop choice, and long-term soil effects remain live design questions.

That is the useful balance: the old loop has evidence behind it, and the
evidence still asks for care.

## The safety no one mentions

Here's a factor that rarely makes it into the conversation, and it deserves to lead: urine is one of the *safest* fertilizers you can handle.

Think about what synthetic fertilizers actually are. Concentrated salts and caustic compounds that come with warnings - wear gloves, avoid skin and eyes, keep away from children, don't inhale the dust. They can chemically burn a plant's roots if you misjudge the dose, and burn your skin too. They're an industrial product, and they behave like one.

Urine, by contrast, comes out of a healthy body essentially sterile, and it's something your own hands have no reaction to. Diluted for the garden, it's gentler to work with than almost anything you can buy. There's no protective gear, no burn risk to your skin, no toxic dust. The whole difficulty of the thing, the *entire* technical challenge, comes down to one modest word: **dilution.** Get the ratio right and you have a plant food that's powerful and forgiving at the same time - a combination the commercial options almost never offer.

## Houseplants know

You don't need a farm to test this. Houseplants are the quiet proof.

Diluted well and watered into the soil, urine feeds potted plants without drama and without smell. People who keep plants this way report the same thing again and again: visitors notice the plants look unusually happy - greener, fuller, more alive - without ever knowing why, and without noticing anything else at all. No odor, no residue, nothing to give it away. Just plants that are thriving on the exact nutrients they'd get from a bottle, delivered by the most local supply chain imaginable.

That's the tell. When something works this well and this invisibly, the only thing keeping it a secret is that nobody expects it to be there.

## Fresh or aged? The one distinction that matters

Before any of the ratios, there's a single thing to understand - the one piece of knowledge that separates a thriving plant from a dead one. It's the difference between *fresh* urine and *aged* urine, and it comes down to one chemical reaction and *where* that reaction happens.

Fresh urine, straight from the body, is mildly acidic, and its nitrogen is bound up in a stable-looking molecule called urea. Urea seems calm. It isn't - it's a loaded spring. The moment fresh urine touches soil, the microbes living there fall on the urea and tear it apart, releasing its nitrogen as ammonia. That reaction happens *in the ground, right up against the roots*, and it happens fast, in a concentrated pulse. Ammonia is alkaline and, at that concentration, toxic to root tissue. It spikes the pH exactly where the plant is most tender.

This is what gardeners mean when they call something **"hot."** Fresh urea is hot the same way fresh manure is hot: not warm to the touch, but chemically fierce - an active reaction that scorches living roots if it goes off in a tight space. Fresh manure has to be composted and "cured" before it's safe to plant into, for precisely this reason. Fresh urine is the same substance-in-waiting. The urea hasn't spent itself yet, and you do not want it discharging that energy inside a pot.

Aged urine has already sprung the spring. Left to sit sealed for a stretch, that very same reaction runs its course *in the jar instead of in the soil.* The urea converts to ammonia in the container, the sharpest excess escapes as gas, and the material settles into something stable - so what you eventually pour out has already done its fierce chemistry somewhere harmless. It's still strong and alkaline, which is exactly why it still needs diluting - but it no longer detonates a fresh reaction against the roots. This is the "cured" version. The sharp smell rising off the jar isn't a warning; it's the proof it worked - that's the ammonia doing its burning in the container instead of in your soil.

Now lay that over the pot-versus-field logic, and every outcome lines up cleanly:

- **Fresh urine in a pot is the danger zone.** The hot reaction fires inside a small enclosed space with nowhere for the ammonia pulse to disperse. This is how a houseplant dies - not from too much fertilizer, but from a chemical burn going off in the root zone. Avoid it completely.
- **Fresh urine in an open field is largely fine** at sensible rates, because the ground disperses the pulse before it can concentrate - the same vast buffering that lets a field shrug off strong doses.
- **Aged, well-diluted urine in a pot is the gentle, reliable choice** - the reaction already over, the material stable, the plant fed without shock. This is what quietly keeps windowsills thriving.

So the rule beneath all the other rules: **for pots and houseplants, use aged urine, well diluted - never fresh.** One killed houseplant is the classic way to learn it, and the lesson it teaches is subtle: the plant didn't die from strength. It died from a hot reaction detonating in a box with no way out.

## How it's actually done

The practice is old and forgiving. The essentials:

**Dilute it.** For established plants in open garden soil, roughly one part urine to ten parts water is a common, safe starting point. For potted plants, houseplants, and young seedlings, go gentler - one part in fifteen to twenty - because a pot can't drain away excess the way open ground can. When unsure, dilute more; you can always feed again.

**Put it on the soil, not the plant.** Water it in around the base, not onto leaves, and not onto the parts of leafy greens you'll eat. The roots are where it belongs.

**Feed during growth.** Every week or two through the growing season is plenty. Ease off when plants are dormant and not hungry.

**Let pots breathe now and then.** Over time, salts can accumulate in containers. An occasional flush with plain water resets them. (One quiet advantage: the more mineral-light your own diet, the milder your urine is on this front to begin with.)

**Keep it simple and single-source.** Urine from a healthy household is the easiest and lowest-risk material to work with. Storing it sealed for a stretch before use raises its alkalinity and helps neutralize anything of concern - a step formalized in sanitation guidelines from groups like the Stockholm Environment Institute, and worth knowing if you're scaling beyond a windowsill.

## "But is it clean?" - the honest answer

Yes, with the obvious care. The two questions people raise, answered plainly:

*Pathogens.* Urine from a healthy person starts nearly sterile, and storage makes it more so. This is not the risky part of human waste - that's the solid stream, which is a separate subject entirely.

*Medications.* Some fraction of pharmaceuticals leaves the body in urine, and this is a fair thing to ask about. The field studies that have looked closely - including work by the Rich Earth Institute in Vermont with university partners - found that while plants take up trace amounts, the levels remaining in the tissue are so small as to be very unlikely to matter for human health. If you want extra margin, favor ornamentals, fruit trees, and non-salad crops over greens eaten raw. But the alarm here is much larger than the evidence.

And the evidence overall is not thin. Controlled field trials have matched urine against synthetic fertilizer across hay, corn, hemp, figs, and flowers and found comparable or better yields. Undiluted urine, applied at sensible rates, didn't scorch the crops that conventional wisdom said it would. Switzerland went so far as to approve a urine-derived fertilizer for use on food crops. The science, in other words, has already quietly agreed with what gardeners keep discovering by hand.

## The only thing in the way

So if it feeds plants as well as the bought stuff, handles more safely, costs nothing, and recycles the one nutrient we're running out of - what's stopping it?

A feeling. That's the whole answer. Not a technical wall, not a safety failure, not a gap in the research. Just disgust, pointed in the wrong direction.

Look at where the revulsion actually fires. It fires at the closed loop - urine returned gently to soil - and stays completely silent about the open one: rock blasted from a pit and shipped across oceans, nitrogen runoff choking rivers into dead zones, the genuinely degraded conditions we've learned to see as clean and normal because they happen out of sight and behind an industrial logo. The feeling isn't tracking harm. It's tracking familiarity. It guards us against the solution and waves the problem right through.

There's something almost funny in it, and the humor is the useful part: we are mining mountains for the thing we flush, and the only reason we don't stop is that stopping feels strange. The strangeness is the entire barrier. It's not load-bearing. It's just a habit wearing the costume of good sense.

## The loop is in your hands

You don't have to overhaul anything to prove this to yourself. A diluted jar and a thirsty plant is the whole experiment, and the plant will tell you the truth within a few weeks - the same truth people keep stumbling onto in their own homes and gardens, the same truth the field trials keep confirming in more formal language.

What you find isn't exotic. It's the oldest arrangement there is: the body feeds the soil, the soil feeds the plant, the plant feeds the body. For nearly all of human history that loop stayed closed, without anyone needing a theory for it. We broke it recently, for reasons that once made sense, and then forgot we'd broken it.

Closing it again is not a sacrifice or a stunt. It's just picking the cycle back up where we set it down - and noticing, a little sheepishly, that it was never really waste. It was fertility the whole time, waiting on the other side of a feeling.

- **Rich Earth Institute** (Brattleboro, Vermont) - the leading U.S. program on urine as fertilizer. Multi-year field trials found urine-fertilized crops matching synthetic-fertilizer yields across hay, corn, hemp, figs, and cut flowers, and that undiluted urine at agronomic rates did not scorch grasses. Their farmer guidance and research summaries: richearthinstitute.org.
- **Pharmaceutical uptake** - studies by the Rich Earth Institute with university partners (including the University of Michigan and University at Buffalo) found that while plants take up trace pharmaceutical residues from urine-derived fertilizer, the amounts remaining in plant tissue are too small to be of likely significance to human health.
- **Aurin** - the world's first urine-derived fertilizer with full regulatory approval, produced via the VUNA process developed at Eawag (Swiss Federal Institute of Aquatic Science and Technology) and commercialized by VUNA / VunaNexus. Approved by the Swiss Federal Office for Agriculture for use on edible plants (2018), and later in Liechtenstein and Austria. eawag.ch; vuna.ch.
- **Pathogen safety & storage** - guidance on sanitizing urine through storage (time and temperature allowing naturally occurring ammonia to inactivate pathogens) traces to the Stockholm Environment Institute and related WHO guidelines on the productive use of excreta.
- **Nutrient content & the phosphorus question** - urine carries the majority of the nitrogen and much of the phosphorus excreted by the body; on "peak phosphorus" and the finite, geographically concentrated nature of mined rock phosphate, see the broader sustainability literature (e.g., Cordell et al. on the global phosphorus challenge).
