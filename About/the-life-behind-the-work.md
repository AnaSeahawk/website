# The Life Behind the Work

*What matters most is a path of heart.*

An independent researcher and explorer, Ana brings a lifelong practice of cultivating, creating, observing and self-study to questions of body, home and habitat. Her writing, workshops and conversations grow from living in different cultures, working with her hands, and discovering what brings pleasure and connection into everyday life.

At the heart of this work is a simple understanding: we already belong to the living world. There are ways to participate wherever we find ourselves.

The story begins in Unity, Saskatchewan, above a small-town pharmacy. Flowers were part of the everyday world too: her mother was a florist, and much of infancy and early childhood unfolded in the back of the flower shop.

Downstairs in the pharmacy, people came for prescriptions and conversation. Lotions were still prepared by hand. Her father was the pharmacist. Visits brought a growing awareness of people looking for something that might help them feel better.

Making things was part of family life. Both parents built and renovated; her father also developed photographs in his own lab. A move from Unity to Yellowknife brought an adventure of its own. There, childhood included time beside him in the basement goldsmith's workshop. He had returned to school to learn a new craft. Gold was cast from wax patterns, then cut, filed and polished. There were repairs too: familiar things passing through skilled hands to be worn again.

Life in Yellowknife extended out onto the lakes. Family flights left from Great Slave Lake outside the house, travelling to a cabin through the seasons. One memory is of watching her father patch an aircraft's fabric where ravens had pecked through. She was comfortable paddling a canoe on her own, and her parents trusted her to camp overnight from a young age. There was room to venture out, long before mobile phones could keep everyone in touch.

Ballet and swimming were favourites. During high school, she worked briefly as a lifeguard and swimming teacher alongside her work in the family jewellery store. When her parents asked what she wanted to do for a career, the answer was simple: "I don't care as long as I'm outside."

That wish eventually led underwater. After leaving Yellowknife, scuba instructor training opened into teaching and running a dive lodge with her husband on the West Coast. Fascination with the underwater environment accompanied the daily work of boats, compressors, tanks and weight belts. During those coastal years she also became a mother. Her son was born in Campbell River in 1998.

The physical demands eventually called for a change. After the first wrist surgery, yoga became a regular part of life. She noticed relief in her back, greater wrist mobility and less swelling in her legs after long days on her feet. The changes drew her back to class six days a week for two years. Practice opened questions that she continued to follow through self-study.

A return to Yellowknife brought her back into the family jewellery business. Diamond grading came first, followed by herbal studies. For about two years, life moved between Yellowknife and Quadra Island. Jewellery design renewed a familiar craft, while the herbal course opened an interest that would grow through years of reading and experience.

She missed the coast too much to stay away. Back around Campbell River and Quadra Island, she drove high-speed water taxis through waters familiar from the diving years. Seymour Narrows, Okisollo Channel and Hole in the Wall were places where powerful tidal currents made timing and attention consequential. The work took her to logging camps, fishing resorts and remote inlets, carrying passengers and freight. Some loads required dangerous-goods training; others involved a helicopter lifting freight from the back deck.

There were night runs, storms and dense fog, with radar part of the work of finding a way through. At times she worked alone, far from help, in places where radio contact was unavailable. Waiting for people to return, she might walk in the forest, carrying a simple awareness: she was the one who had to drive the boat home. Her own condition mattered to everyone depending on that return. Staying calm under pressure was part of the job, and the strain was real.

The work proved even harder on her wrists than diving. A second wrist surgery followed. Her experience with herbal remedies during recovery gave the earlier studies a new personal significance. Learning kept returning to the body and to what she noticed in her own life.

During those coastal years, the former dive lodge also took on another life as a boutique guesthouse. Renovating it brought attention to how a place could work for the people staying there. Her own home on Quadra Island allowed that attention to extend from the ground beneath it to the fixtures and finishes within.

The blueprints were her own, drawn by hand through several drafts. Renovating with both parents and reading had given her a working understanding of structural spans and load-bearing walls. She reviewed the plans with her builder, [John Seffzig](https://www.johnseffzigconstruction.ca/), and participated throughout construction, working directly with him, the plumber and the electrician.

One intention guided the design: "When you're inside, I want it to feel like you're outside."

Site orientation, daylight and views mattered. So did airflow, how the windows opened, and screens that allowed them to remain open. Wood and stone floors, colours, kitchen cupboards, countertops and the particular light fixtures were all considered. Bookcases and the arrangement of the walk-in closet belonged to the same question: how would daily life happen here?

The house stood on a rock bluff. Beneath it, the changing height of the crawlspace was worked into accessible, lit storage, with a full-height entrance at one end and room to reach even the narrowest part. Maintenance access received attention alongside the rooms above. The property needed its own well, wastewater arrangements and an electrical supply brought in. Designing a home meant thinking about what supported it, what moved through it, and how it could be cared for over time.

Later, Christopher Alexander's [pattern language](https://www.patternlanguage.com/archive/grassroots.html) would offer words for relationships she had already been exploring intuitively: the inhabitant's part in shaping a dwelling, and the dialogue through which a way of living becomes a built place. Living design had already been taking shape through decisions, revisions and use.

Then came a year of preparing to leave. In 2012, after selling her home and belongings, she and her partner left Canada. Her son was fourteen. The nomadic life that began then continues today.

There was a privilege in having time for independent study and exploration. Reading accompanied cooking, making, observing and adapting to unfamiliar places. Much of the early journey unfolded away from social media. Ordinary life in different cultures brought its own education: shopping for food, sharing kitchens, communicating across languages, and discovering how differently a room could be arranged. Moving repeatedly made the usefulness of every possession a recurring question.

Early in those travels, running out of a familiar shampoo led to an unexpected discovery. Faced with unfamiliar products, she found a bottle in a Buddhist medicine shop and took it to a friend at a cafe to ask what was in it. He translated the contents as fermented bergamot fruit. That answer sent her into research.

Soon, pineapple and orange peels were fermenting in containers that travelled in her backpack. Eco Enzyme entered daily life through laundry and household cleaning, replacing several purchased products. Something previously discarded had become something she could make and tend. It was a grounding practice that could accompany a change of room or country, and it opened a longer exploration of what happened to food remnants after a meal.

Living in small spaces in George Town, on Penang Island, she made kefir for herself and began experimenting with kefir cheese. Friendships formed around HIN Bus Depot and its weekly market, where she was invited to have a table. She sold kefir milk and cheese, and interest spread by word of mouth. Two women approached her about offering workshops for them in George Town. Something begun for her own enjoyment had become a way of meeting people and sharing what she was learning.

Her workshops ranged from the basics of milk kefir to cheesemaking, alongside water kefir, Bokashi composting and Eco Enzyme. For a time, kombucha was part of that work too. These were practices she lived with herself: cultures to tend, food to prepare, and household remnants whose usefulness continued beyond the meal.

Bokashi became another part of the learning: fermenting kitchen scraps as part of soil building, following material beyond the point at which it would ordinarily leave the household as rubbish. Working with containers made space a practical part of the question. There had to be somewhere for each stage of the work, alongside cooking, washing and the other activities of home.

At the Vivarium in George Town, from late 2016 to early 2020, the apartment itself became a sustained living-design project. It occupied a third-floor corner unit, with large opening windows on three sides. Air conditioning was removed, and the work turned toward inhabiting a tropical climate through the building's existing openings, light and movement of air.

Window coverings provided shade; privacy screens allowed bedroom doors to remain open for airflow. These decisions had to be lived with through ordinary days and nights, alongside guests whose needs also mattered. The attention once given to the windows and orientation of the Quadra house now had another setting: adapting an existing urban apartment to a different climate.

Containers outside the bedroom windows and pots on the balcony held herbs, aloe, moringa trees and other plants. Hanging planters and kokedama extended the garden into the window spaces. A passion-fruit vine found its way onto the laundry wire. Gardening, laundry and the use of a room shared the same limited space.

The Vivarium also made the work available to other people. Guests could join in caring for plants, harvesting and processing moringa, tending fruit-peel ferments and working with household food remnants. Bokashi was part of the apartment's ongoing practice, with voluntary participation extending into the building's community. Questions could begin with something a guest had seen growing, tasted, or watched being prepared.

Over those years, the Vivarium gave Biophilia Living Design a place to develop through repeated use. Light, air, privacy, plants, food and the handling of household remnants had to fit together within a home people shared. It brought the earlier interest in cultivating a living environment into sustained conversation with urban life.

Another part of this exploration unfolded in Luang Prabang, Laos. Larvae had appeared spontaneously in her Bokashi bin around 2018. The first reaction was alarm, followed by curiosity about whether they might have a place in the work. Research led her to identify them as the Phoenix Fly (*Hermetia illucens*, known as the Black Soldier Fly). She spent time learning its life cycle and how to maintain a habitat in which it could continue to be present, beginning with kitchen remnants.

As she became familiar with that cycle, the work extended to her own humanure. In Luang Prabang, she spent about a year developing this practice with the larvae, entirely within containers and without direct access to a ground garden. It brought attention to the material leaving the body as well as the scraps leaving the kitchen. This sustained experience remains part of the background to Soil Makers.

The neighbours' hens brought an unexpected change when they laid eggs in an open bin of dark soil. An incubator followed, and she and her partner raised the chicks by hand. The chickens became pets. Wanting more room for them to roam led to a move from town to a small farm just outside Luang Prabang, where they also tended a small herb garden and moringa trees.

With the lockdown period came a change in the rhythm of travel. The Vivarium had closed, visits to George Town became infrequent, and life centred more on Luang Prabang. Reading and study occupied more of her time. Caring for the chickens also brought decisions about how much could be tended at once; the humanure practice came to an end. When it was time to leave Luang Prabang, much of the electronic equipment accumulated during that stay went to a local school.

These experiences gave Urbaculture its everyday substance: participating in the living processes of an existing urban home. Food comes in; peels remain; plants grow; water passes through. The household is already involved in those relationships. Working with them can begin with something as small as making a drink from whole spices, holding star anise in the hand, recognising its scent and discovering what gives the drink its flavour.

The learning also included mistakes. Becoming ill after misidentifying a plant was a painful encounter with the limits of recognition. Reading and practical experience continued to challenge one another.

Familiar routines were harder to sustain while travelling, and when her wrists troubled her again, access became an immediate concern. Discovering Shivambu carried a particular sense of freedom because the practice was available wherever she was. That experience is part of the personal history behind the research gathered here.

Health became especially precious far from familiar support. Calm, preparation and adaptability remained useful through changing countries, flights, accommodation and the many arrangements of a life on the move. Divorce, homesickness and distance from family are also part of her experience. In 2024 she became a grandmother. Connection continued across that distance, alongside the reality of missing people.

The workshops and conversations grow from these years of making, reading, working and living. Biophilia Living Design brings attention to light, air, materials, plants and how a particular person inhabits a space. The Body as Home carries the questions opened through physical work, injury, movement and practice. Soil Makers and Eco Enzymes bring attention to kitchen scraps, fermentation and the materials of everyday life. Each has a history of being lived with.

People encounter something in that work that interests them and come with their own questions. A conversation might begin with a favourite food, a childhood memory, the last time they enjoyed being outdoors, or the room they live in now. Pleasure and curiosity help determine what is worth exploring together.

Over time, these recurring interests have become a vocation: the work of a life gradually recognised and given form. A window, a plant, a kitchen, a body, a conversation. Each offers a place to participate. The relationship with the living world is already there.
