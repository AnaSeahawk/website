The Life Behind the Work

What matters most is a path of heart.

Ana’s work grows from a lifelong curiosity about how things live, change and take shape. An independent researcher and explorer, a mother and grandmother, she brings that curiosity to the body, the home and the environments we participate in. Her writing and conversations draw on professional studies, practical work and years of learning through everyday life.

In Unity, Saskatchewan, a small-town pharmacy and a flower shop brought plants, preparation and care into her earliest surroundings. Her mother was a florist; her father, a pharmacist who prepared lotions by hand and knew the people coming through the door. Both parents built and renovated. Creativity was something done with materials, spaces and people’s needs.

In Yellowknife, the family goldsmith’s workshop offered another kind of education. Lost-wax casting meant following a piece through successive transformations: a model, a mould, molten metal, then cutting, filing and polishing. Jewellery repair taught attention to what could be preserved and worked again. Diamond-grading studies with the Gemological Institute of America gave precision to that observation, while jewellery design brought technical understanding into creative decisions. Study supplied tools that became familiar through use.

Beyond the workshop were lakes, forest and northern weather. Family flying, canoeing and camping brought freedom alongside responsibility. Ballet and swimming developed another familiarity with movement, followed by work as a lifeguard and swimming instructor. Water became a place of both pleasure and attention.

That relationship deepened through PADI Divemaster training and operating a dive lodge on the West Coast. Boats, compressors, tanks and the underwater environment required preparation and care. Later, operating crew boats and high-speed water taxis brought Marine Emergency Duties (MED A3) training, under Transport Canada’s Marine Safety standards, into daily use. Dangerous-cargo training also supported work transporting explosives for road building in remote areas. Around Campbell River, Quadra Island and the coastal inlets, currents, storms, night passages and dense fog made timing consequential. Remote work required calm judgment and an awareness that other people depended on her ability to bring the boat home.

The physical demands also brought the body’s limits into focus. Yoga became a sustained practice, opening a different relationship with movement and recovery. Chartered Herbalist studies at Dominion Herbal College in British Columbia, Canada, provided a foundation that continued to grow through reading, observation and practical experience, eventually extending into Ayurveda. Questions encountered in daily life gave direction to the study; experience continually brought new questions back to it.

Design carried the same movement between understanding and application. Renovating a dive lodge into a guesthouse meant considering how a place would be lived in by different people. Designing her own home on Quadra Island extended that attention into hand-drawn blueprints, structural spans and load-bearing walls. Working with builder John Seffzig, and directly with the plumber and electrician, she participated throughout its construction.

“When you’re inside, I want it to feel like you’re outside” was the intention. Orientation, daylight, airflow and opening windows mattered alongside wood and stone floors, lighting, cupboards and storage. The rock bluff beneath the house, its well, wastewater arrangements and maintenance access were all part of the design. Later, Christopher Alexander’s pattern language offered words for relationships already being explored through those decisions.

Nomadic living brought this inquiry into existing spaces across different cultures. Shared kitchens, unfamiliar ingredients and rooms arranged around other ways of life became a continuing education. Moving repeatedly also made minimalism practical: what was useful, what could be cared for, and what was worth carrying?

Fermentation became one answer. A discovery of fermented fruit preparations led to making Eco Enzyme from fruit peels for household use. Milk kefir developed into cheesemaking; interest at a market in George Town, Penang, grew into invitations to teach. Workshops followed in kefir, cheesemaking, water kefir, Bokashi and Eco Enzyme. These were practices already woven into daily life, becoming opportunities to learn with others.

At the Vivarium in George Town, an urban apartment became a place to explore living design through use. Shade, opening windows and airflow supported life without air conditioning. Herbs, moringa and hanging plants shared space with cooking, laundry and guests. Light, privacy, food and the handling of household remnants had to work together. In Luang Prabang, container-based experiments with Bokashi, humanure and the Phoenix Fly (Hermetia illucens, known as the Black Soldier Fly) extended that attention to what leaves a household and how it enters other living processes.

These experiences give Urbaculture and Biophilia Living Design their practical ground. Connection with nature can be present in tending a plant, preparing food, understanding a material or changing how a room is used. An existing home offers many places to participate.

Shivambu has become a particularly compelling part of this continuing exploration. Her personal practice with the body’s own waters underlies Sovereign Biophysics, bringing sustained observation into conversation with Ayurveda, alchemy and independent research. It is the newest expression of her work and the inquiry she is most passionate about sharing.

The workshops and private conversations grow from this range of experience. A question about the body may meet a history of physical work and practice; a question about home may meet years of designing, hosting and adapting to different places. The starting point is the person, their curiosity, and the life they are actually living.
