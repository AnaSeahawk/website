The Life Behind the Work

What matters most is a path of heart.

An independent researcher and explorer, Ana brings a lifelong practice of cultivating, creating, observing and self-study to questions of body, home and habitat. Her writing, Circles and conversations grow from living in different cultures, working with her hands, and discovering what brings pleasure and connection into everyday life.

At the heart of this work is a simple understanding: we already belong to the living world. There are ways to participate wherever we find ourselves.

The story begins in Unity, Saskatchewan, above a small-town pharmacy. Her father was the pharmacist. Lotions were still prepared by hand, and he knew the people who came in looking for something that might help them feel better. Her mother was a florist, and infancy and early childhood unfolded in the back of the flower shop. Both parents built and renovated. Remedies, flowers and rooms being remade all belonged to the same ordinary world.

A move north to Yellowknife brought an adventure of its own. Her father had gone back to school to become a goldsmith, and the basement became his workshop. The method was lost-wax casting. Each piece was first made in wax, the waxes were joined into a small tree, and the tree was set in plaster and fired in the kiln until the wax ran out. Gold was melted in the crucible and spun into the space the wax had left. Then came the saw, the file and the polishing wheel, and repairs too: familiar things passing through skilled hands to be worn again. She worked beside him and made jewellery of her own. Years later she returned to the family business to design, and studied diamond grading with the Gemological Institute of America, learning to read colour, clarity and cut through a loupe.

The north was also lake and sky. Family flights left from Great Slave Lake, outside the house, for a cabin visited through the seasons. She paddled a canoe on her own and camped overnight from a young age, long before mobile phones could keep everyone in touch. When her parents asked what she wanted to do for a career, the answer was simple: “I don’t care as long as I’m outside.”

Water came next. Ballet and swimming were the childhood favourites, and there was a spell as a lifeguard and swimming teacher. Then the West Coast, PADI Divemaster training, and a dive lodge to run, where the days were boats, compressors, tanks and weight belts, and every dive was timed to the tide. Later she drove crew boats and high-speed water taxis through Seymour Narrows, Okisollo Channel and Hole in the Wall, carrying passengers and freight to logging camps, fishing resorts and remote inlets. Marine Emergency Duties and a dangerous-goods ticket came with the job; some loads were dynamite for road building, and some were lifted from the back deck by helicopter. There were night runs, storms and dense fog on radar, often alone and beyond radio contact. A certificate opened the door. What stayed was a simple awareness: she was the one who had to bring the boat home.

During those coastal years she became a mother.

The work was heavy, and her wrists and back carried it. Yoga brought relief in her back, more movement in her wrists and less swelling in her legs after long days on her feet, and it drew her to class six days a week for two years. A year of Chartered Herbalist studies with Dominion Herbal College, some 600 hours, gave her the body’s systems and the plants of the materia medica. Herbal remedies became personal during a recovery of her own, and herbalism has grown into a sustained study of Ayurveda.

Rooms were another kind of study. The former dive lodge was renovated into a guesthouse. Then came her own house on Quadra Island, on a rock bluff, from blueprints she drew by hand through several drafts and built with John Seffzig. One intention guided it: “When you’re inside, I want it to feel like you’re outside.” Orientation and daylight mattered. So did how the windows opened, the wood and stone underfoot, the light fixtures, the well, the wastewater and a crawlspace that could be reached to its narrowest corner. Christopher Alexander’s pattern language later offered words for what she had been doing by intuition.

Then the house and nearly everything in it were sold, and a nomadic life began that continues today. It has been spent living as local people live: shopping for food, sharing kitchens, changing languages, living inside other people’s ideas of a room. Early on, a familiar shampoo ran out. In a Buddhist medicine shop she found a bottle she could not read, and a friend at a cafe translated the contents: fermented bergamot fruit. Soon pineapple and orange peels were fermenting in containers that travelled in her backpack, and Eco Enzyme was washing the clothes and the dishes.

In George Town, on Penang Island, kefir made for herself became kefir cheese, then a table at the weekly market at Hin Bus Depot. Two women asked whether she would teach, and the teaching began: kefir, cheese, Bokashi, Eco Enzyme. At the Vivarium, a third-floor apartment with windows on three sides, the air conditioning came out and a tropical home was made from shade, screens and moving air. Moringa and herbs grew at the windows, and a passion-fruit vine found the laundry wire. Guests arrived through Airbnb, where she was a Superhost, and many joined in the harvesting and the ferments.

In Luang Prabang, Laos, larvae appeared in the Bokashi bin. Alarm gave way to curiosity. They were the Phoenix Fly (Hermetia illucens, known as the Black Soldier Fly), and for a year she worked with them to turn kitchen remnants and her own humanure into soil, entirely in containers. The neighbours’ hens chose that dark soil to lay their eggs in. An incubator followed, then hand-raised chicks, then a small farm outside town with herbs and moringa to tend.

That attention to rooms now carries into Place Readings, where a home and the life happening within it are considered together.

Far from familiar routines, her wrists troubled her again. Discovering Shivambu carried a particular sense of freedom, because the practice was available wherever she was. That story is told in its own place.

She is a grandmother now, and family is a long way off. Missing people is part of this life too.

People come across something here that interests them and arrive with their own questions. A conversation might begin with a favourite food, a childhood memory, the last time they enjoyed being outdoors, or the room they live in now.

A window, a plant, a kitchen, a body, a conversation. Each offers a place to participate. The relationship with the living world is already there.
